Module 8 • Lesson 1📋 The Brief & the Plan

The capstone begins here — not in Photoshop, but on paper. Professionals do not start with a canvas; they start with a brief that defines what success looks like, and a plan that maps the route. This lesson turns "make something cool" into a project you can actually finish and be proud of.

📚 What You'll Learn

By the end of this lesson, you will be able to:

  • Write a tight creative brief: objective, audience, deliverables, constraints, success criteria
  • Translate a vague idea into specific, checkable requirements
  • Build a moodboard that fixes the visual direction before you start
  • Draft a staged plan that maps each phase to the skills you have learned
  • Scope realistically so the project is ambitious but finishable

⏱️ Estimated Time: 45 minutes

🎯 Project: A one-page brief, a moodboard, and a staged plan for your self-directed capstone.

👤 The Goal: A Spec, Not a Wish

🧠 Mental Model: The Brief Is the Contract

A brief is the agreement between the vision and the work — with a client, or just with your future self at 2 a.m. wondering if you are done. Its job is to make success checkable. "Make it modern" cannot be checked; "a 3000×2000 hero image for a coffee brand's homepage, warm and editorial, readable at a glance on mobile, delivered as web + print by Friday" can.

The discipline that makes a brief work is specificity. Every vague adjective should be pinned to something concrete: a reference image, a measurable spec, a named constraint. When you can point at the brief and say "done — here is each requirement met", you have escaped the infinite-revision trap.

🧾 Anatomy of a Brief

A one-page brief has six parts. Keep it short — a page forces the clarity that three pages let you avoid.

  • Objective — the one sentence on what this must achieve and why.
  • Audience — who it is for; their context and what should make them stop.
  • Deliverables + specs — exact outputs, sizes, formats, and where they will live.
  • Constraints — brand rules, colors, fonts, budget, and the deadline.
  • Success criteria — the checkable tests that mean "done and good".
  • References / moodboard — images that fix tone, so words like "bold" have a picture.

The moodboard deserves its own moment. Before designing, gather 6–12 references — photography, grading, type, layout — that capture the target feel, and note why each is there. It aligns you (and a client) on direction while it is still cheap to change, and it becomes the yardstick you grade your drafts against.

🗺️ The Plan: Map Skills to Stages

With the brief fixed, the plan is almost mechanical: break the build into stages and name which of your skills each one uses. This capstone is deliberately a chance to touch the whole course, so a typical plan reads like the syllabus in reverse:

⚠️ Scope for finishing, not for showing off

The commonest capstone failure is over-scoping. A tightly executed simple idea beats an ambitious half-finished one every time. Pick something you can complete to a high polish in your available time, then add stretch goals you can cut without breaking the deliverable.

🛠️ Guided Build: Brief, Moodboard, Plan

Decide on a project. A strong capstone is a single, real-feeling deliverable: a movie-style key art poster, a product campaign hero, an editorial portrait spread, an album cover.

Step 1: Write the brief · 15 min

  1. Fill the six parts on one page. Force every adjective to a spec or a reference.
  2. Write the success criteria as a checklist you could hand to someone else to grade.

Step 2: Build the moodboard · 15 min

  1. Collect 6–12 references for tone, color, type, and composition. A contact-sheet PSD or a simple grid is fine.
  2. Annotate each with the one thing you are taking from it.

Step 3: Draft the plan · 10 min

  1. List the stages and the module skill each uses. Note the assets you must source or shoot (free Stock, your own photos).
  2. Set two or three milestones and a realistic finish date. Mark stretch goals as optional. 🏆

✅ Project Completion Checklist

  • ☐ A one-page brief with all six parts
  • ☐ Every vague term pinned to a spec or reference
  • ☐ Success criteria written as a checkable list
  • ☐ An annotated moodboard of 6–12 references
  • ☐ A staged plan mapping skills, assets, and milestones

🧗 Now You: Pick Your Project

🌟 Choose one to brief

  1. Key art poster — a composited hero with title treatment (touches Modules 1, 3, 4, 5).
  2. Product campaign — a hero plus a sized social set from one master (Modules 2, 4, 5, 6).
  3. Editorial portrait — a retouched, graded portrait with layout and type (Modules 2, 3, 5).
  4. Your own — anything that lets you show the skills you most want on your portfolio.

Going further: write the brief as if a real client sent it, then a second version as your own artist statement. Compare — client work and personal work reward different success criteria.

🍳 Recipe Card: The Brief

Idea → brief → plan

  1. Six parts: objective · audience · deliverables+specs · constraints · success · references
  2. Pin every adjective to a spec or a moodboard image
  3. Write success criteria as a checkable list
  4. Stage the plan; map each stage to a module skill
  5. Scope to finish; make stretch goals cuttable

Mantra: if you can't check it, it isn't in the brief.

How long should a capstone take?

Scope it to your time, not the calendar. A focused weekend can produce a portfolio-grade single deliverable; a campaign set might take a week of evenings. The plan's milestones are there so you can tell whether you're on track and cut stretch goals early if not.

What if I don't have a client or a real brief?

Invent one — a fictional brand, a favorite band's next album, a film that doesn't exist. Self-authored briefs are how most portfolios are built. Just hold yourself to the same specificity a client would demand.
