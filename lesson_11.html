Module 4 • Lesson 2💡 Light, Shadows & Reflections

Perspective puts an element in the right place; light makes it real. You match the scene's light direction, quality, and color on the element itself, then anchor it to the world with the shadows and reflections the eye demands before it will believe anything.

📚 What You'll Learn

By the end of this lesson, you will be able to:

  • Read a scene's light: direction, quality (hard/soft), and color
  • Relight an element with dodge & burn and masked color so it matches
  • Build a believable cast shadow (transform, blur, gradient opacity)
  • Add the tight contact shadow that actually glues feet to the floor
  • Fake a convincing reflection on a glossy surface

⏱️ Estimated Time: 60 minutes

🎯 Project: Take the placed element from Lesson 4.1 and light it, shadow it, and (if the ground is glossy) reflect it, so it is undeniably standing in the scene.

👤 The Goal: Anchored to the Ground

Same object, same spot. On the right it floats — no shadow, so the brain reads it as a sticker pasted on top. On the left, a soft contact shadow and a cast shadow running away from the light plant it firmly on the floor. Drag the handle:

🧠 Mental Model: Read the Light First

Before you touch the element, interrogate the background's light. Three questions: Where is it coming from (look at existing shadows — they point away from the source)? How hard is it (crisp shadow edges mean hard, direct sun; soft edges mean an overcast or diffused source)? What color is it (warm afternoon sun, cool shade, a green cast from foliage)?

Whatever you read, the element must obey. If the scene's shadows are soft and fall to the right, your element's own shading and its new shadow must be soft and fall to the right too. A mismatch here is the second most common composite tell after scale.

🌑 Building a Cast Shadow

A cast shadow is its own layer beneath the element. The reliable build: fill the element's silhouette with black on a new layer, flip/skew it away from the light with Free Transform so it lies on the ground, then blur it more the farther it gets from the object and fade its opacity with distance. Real shadows are crisp and dark where they touch the object and soft and pale where they end.

⚠️ Shadows are colored, not black

Pure black shadows look cut-out. Outdoor shadows lean cool/blue because they are lit by the sky, not the sun; indoors they pick up the color of the room's bounced light. Tint the shadow toward whatever color the scene's real shadows have and pull its opacity back to match the depth of the real shadows already in the scene.

🪞 Contact Shadows & Reflections

The contact shadow (or ambient occlusion) is the small, dark, barely-blurred smudge exactly where the object meets the ground. It is the single most convincing element in the whole composite — the eye reads it as physical contact. Even when a long cast shadow is subtle, a good contact shadow sells the grounding.

On a glossy floor, water, or a polished table you also owe a reflection: duplicate the element, flip it vertically, align its base to the object's base, drop the opacity, and add a slight vertical blur that increases with distance. Mask it to fade as it recedes.

✅ The two-shadow habit

Always build both a contact shadow and a cast shadow. They do different jobs: the cast shadow tells you where the light is; the contact shadow tells you the object is touching the world. Skip either and something feels subtly wrong even if you can't name it.

🛠️ Guided Build: Light & Anchor

Open your placed-element file from Lesson 4.1.

Step 1: Read and match the light · 12 min

  1. Note the direction, hardness, and color of the scene's existing shadows.
  2. On the element, add a 50% gray Dodge & Burn layer (Lesson 3.2) clipped to it: brighten the side facing the light, deepen the side away from it, so its shading matches.
  3. Add a clipped Color Balance or Photo Filter to push the element's light color toward the scene's (warm sun, cool shade).

Step 2: Contact shadow · 8 min

  1. New layer under the element, Multiply. With a small soft black brush at low flow, darken exactly where the base meets the ground. Keep it tight and only lightly blurred.

Step 3: Cast shadow · 12 min

  1. Load the element's silhouette as a selection: Ctrl/⌘-click its layer mask thumbnail (or the layer thumbnail if it has no mask). Create a new layer below the element, fill the selection with a very dark color (Edit ▸ Fill), deselect, and set the layer to Multiply. Filling a new layer works even when the cut-out uses a layer mask or is a Smart Object, where Lock Transparent Pixels and a direct fill would not.
  2. Free Transform ▸ Distort/Skew it onto the ground, running away from the light. To make the blur grow with distance, use Filter ▸ Blur Gallery ▸ Tilt-Shift or Field Blur (sharp pins near the object, strong blur at the far end), or blur a duplicate heavily and blend the two with a gradient mask. Then mask a gradient so the shadow fades out, and tint it to match the scene's shadows (cool/blue outdoors).

Step 4: Reflection (if glossy) · 8 min

  1. Duplicate the element, Edit ▸ Transform ▸ Flip Vertical, align bases, lower opacity to ~25–40%, add Motion Blur or Gaussian Blur that increases downward (Blur Gallery, or a blurred copy blended in with a gradient mask), and mask a fade.
  2. Save the layered file (m4-key-visual.psd). You reopen it in Lesson 4.3. 🏆

✅ Project Completion Checklist

  • ☐ Element re-lit to match the scene's direction, hardness, and color
  • ☐ Tight contact shadow at the base (Multiply)
  • ☐ Cast shadow running away from the light, blurred + faded with distance
  • ☐ Shadows tinted (not pure black) to match the scene
  • ☐ Reflection added if the surface is glossy

🧗 Now You: Solo Variation

🌟 Your challenge

  1. Composite the same object into a hard-sun scene and a soft-overcast scene. Feel how completely the shadow's edge and depth have to change.
  2. Put an object on a puddle or a marble floor and build the reflection. Get the base alignment perfect — a reflection that starts a few pixels off is instantly wrong.
  3. Light one element with a colored source (a neon sign, a sunset) and let that color spill onto its lit edge. Colored rim light is what makes advertising composites sing.

Going further: add a subtle rim light — a thin bright edge where the light wraps the element — on a masked layer. It separates the subject from the background and reads as expensive.

🍳 Recipe Card: Light & Shadow

Match the light, anchor the element

  1. Read the scene: light direction · hardness · color
  2. Relight the element (clipped D&B + color) to agree
  3. Contact shadow: tight, dark, low blur, Multiply
  4. Cast shadow: skew away from light, blur + fade with distance, tint to the scene (cool outdoors)
  5. Reflection on glossy floors: flip, align base, fade + blur

Mantra: the contact shadow says "touching," the cast shadow says "where the light is."

📝 Lesson Summary

🎓 Key Takeaways

  • Read the scene's light first: direction, hardness, and color. The element must obey all three.
  • Relight the element with clipped dodge & burn and a clipped color adjustment.
  • Always build two shadows: a tight contact shadow for touch and a cast shadow that runs away from the light, blurring and fading with distance.
  • Shadows take the scene's color, never pure black; glossy surfaces also owe a faded, blurred reflection.

🎉 What You've Accomplished

Your element no longer just sits in the right place; it is lit by the same light as everything around it and touches the ground. Contact and cast shadows are the details viewers never consciously notice and always miss when they are gone, and you can now build both on purpose.

❓ Common Questions at This Stage

Can't I just use the Drop Shadow layer style?

Not as it comes. A layer-style drop shadow sits at a fixed offset and never lies on the ground in perspective, so it reads as graphic design, not photography. You can use it as a starting point: Layer ▸ Layer Style ▸ Create Layer splits the shadow onto its own layer, which you can then distort onto the ground, blur, and mask like any hand-built shadow.

How dark should my shadow be?

Match the scene. Sample the darkness of the shadows already in the background and aim for the same, then usually pull back 5–10%. Too dark is the giveaway; real shadows carry bounced light and are rarely pure black.
