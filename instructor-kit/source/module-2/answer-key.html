L04 · Color Management & Working Spaces (2.1)

Q1
An image is "color poured into a container." Which two properties define that container?
B) Working space (how wide the gamut is) and bit depth (how finely each channel is divided)
Why: The lesson's mental model: the working space sets how wide the container is (which colors it can describe), and bit depth sets how finely it is divided (256 steps per channel in 8-bit, over 32,000 in 16-bit). Format, pixel size and layer count (A, C) are unrelated; the monitor (D) displays the container, it doesn't define it.
Q2
You apply the same aggressive Curves to an 8-bit and a 16-bit copy of a smooth sky. Only the 8-bit copy breaks into bands. Why?
D) The edit demands more tonal steps than 8-bit's 256 per channel; 16-bit has about 128 times more
Why: Banding is what happens when an edit demands more tonal steps than the file has. Bit depth is independent of pixel count (A) and of working space (B): you can have 8-bit ProPhoto or 16-bit sRGB. There is no such setting as C.
Q3
A learner's graded photo looks duller on Instagram than in Photoshop. What is the most likely cause?
A) The file was left in Adobe RGB or ProPhoto and never Converted to sRGB
Why: The lesson's FAQ: a wide-gamut file shown in an sRGB world. Its numbers get read as sRGB, so the colors look muted. Convert to sRGB before exporting for the web and it matches. Low quality (B) causes blockiness, not dullness; 16-bit (C) and Preserve (D) are the recommended settings.
Q4
It is time to deliver a web JPEG from your layered, 16-bit Adobe RGB master. Which is the correct move?
C) On a merged duplicate: Edit ▸ Convert to Profile ▸ sRGB, 8 Bits/Channel, then export
Why: Convert remaps the colors so they keep their appearance; Assign (A) only relabels the container and shifts every color. The lesson makes the web copy with Image ▸ Duplicate (Duplicate Merged Layers Only) so the master stays wide and 16-bit (B and D both damage the master).
Q5
Before any edit, the Histogram shows a tall spike slammed against the right wall. What does it tell you?
B) Highlights are clipped: detail at the bright end is lost
Why: A spike against either wall means clipped tones and lost detail. Underexposure (A) crams the data left; a flat image (C) is a narrow histogram with empty ends. A bright high-key scene (D) sits to the right but should not pile up against the wall.
Q6
Which pairing of working space and job matches the lesson's table?
D) ProPhoto RGB for heavy RAW grading and archival masters, always paired with 16-bit
Why: sRGB is for web, social and email; Adobe RGB for print (it holds cyans and greens sRGB clips); ProPhoto for heavy RAW grading and masters, and always with 16-bit or it bands. A pairs ProPhoto with the two things it must never meet: the web and 8-bit.
Lab reference: Set Up & Diagnose (l04-smooth-sky-16bit.tif, l04-histogram-a…e.jpg, or own image)

A correct result looks like: Edit ▸ Color Settings reopened shows the learner's deliberate choice (Adobe RGB (1998) or sRGB), a CMYK profile, RGB policy Preserve Embedded Profiles and the mismatch boxes ticked. The working file's tab reads RGB/16. A one-sentence diagnosis is written in the workbook before any adjustment layer exists. Two sky windows side by side after the same hard Curves: the 8-bit copy shows distinct horizontal stripes in the upper sky at 100%, the 16-bit one a smooth gradient. color-master.psd is still 16-bit and wide; the exported JPEG is sRGB, 8-bit.

Common wrong results and why:

  • No visible banding. The Curves move was gentle, the view is zoomed out, or the "8-bit" copy was never converted (check the tab). Push the curve's ends far inward and add a second Curves.
  • Both copies band. They converted the original to 8-bit first and duplicated afterward. Reopen the TIFF.
  • Colors jumped at export. Assign Profile instead of Convert to Profile. Undo and Convert.
  • Master is now 8-bit sRGB. The conversion was done on the master and saved. Re-open the practice file (or their own original) and rebuild; stress Image ▸ Duplicate.
  • Diagnosis names a tool, not a problem ("add Curves"). Ask: "What's wrong in the histogram?" Aim for "The tones are crammed left and nothing is near white; it needs lifting."

How to check it fast: look at the document tab (mode, bit depth, and an asterisk or "#" for a profile mismatch in some versions), and at Edit ▸ Convert to Profile: the Source Space line names the file's current profile.

Lab reference: Now You (histogram drill + two-way export + template)

Histogram answers (also in labs/module-2/instructor/l04-histogram-answers.txt): a flat / low contrast (narrow, empty ends) · b high-key snow scene, correctly exposed (piled right but not against the wall: the trap) · c underexposed (crammed left) · d overexposed with clipped highlights (spike on the right wall) · e crushed shadows (spike on the left wall).

Two-way export: the Converted copy of l04-lagoon-adobergb.jpg looks like the original in the browser; the profile-stripped copy is visibly duller, most in the turquoise water, the palm fronds and the "green" and "cyan" swatches. The gray swatch barely changes. A good answer names where the shift showed most. Template: File ▸ New preset saved with 16 bit and Adobe RGB (1998) or ProPhoto RGB under Advanced Options.

Common wrong results: calling b overexposed; stripping the profile from an sRGB copy and seeing "no difference" (the lesson warns this happens because browsers assume sRGB); a template in ProPhoto at 8-bit.

L05 · Grading with Curves, Gradient Maps & LUTs (2.2)

Q1
What separates grading from correcting, in the lesson's words?
C) Correcting asks "what is wrong?"; grading asks "what should it feel like?" and deliberately remaps tones to chosen colors
Why: Every grade is a remap: you decide what color each tone becomes (teal shadows, gold highlights). The lesson's pro grade barely touches exposure (B) and works with Curves, Gradient Maps and LUTs, not only Camera Raw (A). Correcting comes first to build the neutral base (D).
Q2
You want the classic teal-and-gold in Curves. In the Blue channel, what do you do?
A) Lift the shadow end and drop the highlight end
Why: Lifting the shadow end adds blue to the shadows; dropping the highlight end removes blue from the highlights, which reads as warm yellow. For a truer teal, also lift Green slightly or drop Red slightly in the shadows. B gives yellow shadows and blue highlights, the opposite look.
Q3
Your teal-to-gold Gradient Map looks garish and has flattened all the detail. What does the lesson tell you to do?
D) Set the layer to Color or Soft Light and/or drop its opacity to about 15–40%
Why: "Blend it, don't bake it." At 100% Normal a Gradient Map replaces every tone with a ramp color. Lower opacity (the recipe says about 20–30%) or a Color / Soft Light blend lends its palette to the real image underneath.
Q4
Before File ▸ Export ▸ Color Lookup Tables, why do you Shift-click each mask thumbnail and hide non-grade layers?
B) The export bakes every visible adjustment layer, and a LUT cannot store a mask
Why: A LUT is one global input-to-output color recipe; it has no idea where a mask was. The export bakes all visible adjustment layers (not just selected ones, so D is wrong), so switch off the masks and hide anything that isn't the grade, then re-enable the masks afterward.
Q5
Your LUT looks perfect on the shot you built it on but wrong on another photo. What is the most likely reason?
A) The second photo wasn't corrected to a neutral base first, and a LUT assumes a similar starting point
Why: Straight from the lesson FAQ: correct each image to a neutral base first (in the Lesson 2.1 pipeline), then apply the LUT. A LUT is a starting point, not a finisher, and it certainly travels to other images (B); that's its whole purpose.
Q6
Which order does the lesson's flowchart and build follow?
C) Correct to neutral → contrast (S-curve) → color → refine locally with masks → bottle as a LUT
Why: Correct, then grade, then bottle. Contrast is set before color (the checklist's first item), local refinement uses Module 1 masks, and the LUT is exported last, from a grade built on a neutral base.
Lab reference: Grade & Bottle It (l05-portrait-studio.jpg or own portrait → l05-pier-dusk.jpg)

A correct result looks like: grade-working.psd, 16-bit, with (bottom to top) the photo as Background, a neutralizing Curves or Camera Raw correction (the gray card's middle patch reads roughly equal R, G and B in the Info panel), a "contrast" Curves with a gentle S, a color Curves with the Blue shadow end lifted and highlight end dropped (optionally a small Green lift or Red drop in the shadows), a teal-to-gold Gradient Map in Color or Soft Light at about 20–30%, and at least one mask keeping the grade off the face or the highlights. A .cube exists, and a Color Lookup layer on the pier gives it the same teal shadows and warm highlights.

Common wrong results and why:

  • The grade looks muddy-green on the portrait. Teal was laid over the uncorrected yellow cast. Correct to neutral first.
  • Pier looks wildly different from the portrait's look. A stray layer (or the portrait's heavy correction) was visible at export and got baked in. Re-export with only the grade visible.
  • The LUT seems "weaker" than the portrait. Expected when the portrait's grade relied on masks: a LUT can't store them. Discuss it; it's the lesson's point.
  • Masks still disabled in the saved master (red X on the thumbnails). Shift-click to re-enable, save.
  • Export Color Lookup Tables unavailable or failing. The document isn't a Background + adjustment layers structure. Rebuild it that way; meanwhile use the fallback LUT for the transfer test.

How to check it fast: toggle the Gradient Map's eye: if the change is dramatic, it's too strong. Load the learner's .cube onto l05-grade-test-chart.png: the gray ramp should turn teal at the dark end and warm at the bright end, with the mids near neutral.

Lab reference: Now You (three LUTs, reverse-engineered look, one LUT across five photos)

A correct result looks like: three .cube files whose recipes differ in all three dimensions the journal asks about. Typical answers: warm summer = warm/golden highlights, slightly warm shadows, moderate contrast; cold thriller = cyan-blue shadows, cool neutral highlights, higher contrast, often slightly desaturated; faded matte = lifted black point (the RGB curve's shadow end raised), lowered contrast, often a gentle warm or green cast. Across five photos the learner should report at least one where the LUT needed per-image correction first ("a LUT is a starting point, not a finisher").

Common wrong results: three LUTs that are the same look at different strengths; a faded look built by lowering opacity instead of lifting the black point; no neutral base before applying the LUT to the five photos.

L06 · Match, Split-Tone & Soft-Proof (2.3)

Q1
What is the most flattering, cinematic split-tone according to the lesson?
B) Cool shadows, warm highlights, with the mids kept roughly neutral
Why: Cool shadows and warm highlights mimic warm sunlight and cool skylight, which is how we expect the world to look. Neutral mids keep skin believable. Green/magenta (D) is the lesson's deliberately unusual pairing for the solo variation.
Q2
After split-toning, the skin looks ill. What is going on and what is the fix?
D) The color is reaching the midtones where skin lives: keep the split in the extremes, ease the amount, and mask the grade off the face if needed
Why: The lesson's FAQ answer. Skin lives in the mids, so color pushed there reads as sickly. Preserve Luminosity (C) is what the Recipes II split-tone card says to keep ticked; it stops Color Balance from shifting brightness.
Q3
You are matching a companion photo to a reference with Curves. What order does the lesson give?
A) Tones first (brightness and contrast), then black point, white point and mid color from the sampled values
Why: "Match tones before colors": a color match laid over mismatched exposure never holds. Then black point, white point and mid color, in that order. Image ▸ Adjustments ▸ Match Color (D) exists for a quick automatic pass, but hand-matching with Curves is more controllable.
Q4
Where do you read the RGB values of the points you dropped with the Color Sampler Tool?
C) The Info panel (Window ▸ Info)
Why: Color Sampler points report their values in the Info panel. Comparing the two images' readouts tells you which way each channel must move.
Q5
With soft-proofing on, Gamut Warning (Ctrl/⌘ + Shift + Y) grays out a neon sign. What do you do?
B) Lower the saturation there with a masked Hue/Saturation layer until the warning clears
Why: Gray means the press can't reproduce that color. Pulling those areas' saturation down (a masked Hue/Saturation) makes the print match the soft-proof. Assigning a profile (C) just shifts colors; turning off paper simulation (D) hides the problem instead of solving it.
Q6
Which delivery matches the Module 2 deliverable?
D) Keep the layered master wide + 16-bit; from merged duplicates, Convert to sRGB (8-bit JPG) for web and to the printer's profile (PDF or TIFF, or tagged RGB if the printer asks) for print
Why: One look, delivered correctly for two destinations, always from duplicates so the master stays wide and 16-bit. A, B and C each damage the master or skip a destination; B also uses Assign, which relabels instead of converting.
Lab reference: Split-Tone & Proof, the Module 2 deliverable (finish-working.psd, l06-companion-portrait.jpg, l06-neon-proof.jpg)

A correct result looks like: finish-working.psd (still RGB/16, wide space) with a Color Balance layer whose Shadows values lean Cyan and Blue and whose Highlights lean Red and Yellow, Midtones at or near 0; skin reads warmly lit. The companion portrait has a Curves layer that lifts the cast: its gray card's middle patch in the Info panel reads close to the reference's (practice file: before correction the companion's middle patch is strongly blue, roughly R below G below B; after, the three values sit within a few levels of each other and of the reference). The proof setup names a printer profile or SWOP v2, an intent, BPC and Simulate Paper Color; any gamut warnings were handled with a masked Hue/Saturation layer. Two delivered files: an sRGB 8-bit JPEG, and a PDF or TIFF converted to the printer/CMYK profile (or tagged RGB if the printer asked for it).

Common wrong results and why:

  • Skin looks orange or gray-green. The Color Balance moves were made on Midtones (the default Tone). Zero Midtones, redo on Shadows and Highlights.
  • Companion "matched" but still looks different. Channels were moved before brightness and contrast were matched, or the samplers sit on non-comparable points.
  • No gamut warning at all. On a gently graded portrait that can be correct. Confirm proof is on (tab shows the simulated profile) and have them run Step 3 on l06-neon-proof.jpg: the magenta OPEN, the blue arrow, the green arc and probably parts of the orange tube gray out; brick, door and poster don't.
  • Whole-image Hue/Saturation. The neon clears, but the whole picture goes dull. Mask it to the flagged areas.
  • Only one file delivered, or the master converted. The deliverable is two files from duplicates; the master stays wide and 16-bit.

How to check it fast: open the two delivered files and look at the tab (RGB/8 and CMYK/8 or the printer's space); in the master, Edit ▸ Convert to Profile's Source Space should still show the wide working space.

Lab reference: Now You (three-photo set, two split-tones, vivid soft-proof)

A correct result looks like: l06-set-b-tungsten and l06-set-c-shade matched to l06-set-a-daylight: the gray cards read neutral and alike, the linen is off-white in all three, the mug is the same blue, the lemon the same yellow. Tungsten needed Blue raised and Red lowered plus brightening; shade needed Blue lowered and a stronger black point (it is lifted and flat). The green/magenta split-tone should feel unsettling or surreal next to the cool/warm one; the learner names the emotion. The vivid-image proof entry names the areas flagged and roughly how much saturation came out.

Common wrong results: matching only by eye with a global Photo Filter; ignoring the shade frame's lifted blacks; a green/magenta split that is so strong it reads as a color cast rather than a split.
