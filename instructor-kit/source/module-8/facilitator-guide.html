Instructor Kit · Facilitator Guide

Module 8: Capstone

How to run the course finale: one self-directed professional project, taken from a one-page brief to a presented case study. There are no new tools. Your job shifts from demonstrator to studio coach and editor: hold learners to a checkable brief, keep scope finishable, protect the file discipline under deadline pressure, and run a critique that is honest, kind, and specific.

3 lessons Session 8 · 3 hrs incl. break Lessons L21–L23 (8.1–8.3) Level Intermediate L21 taught in the Session 7 kickoff Most of the build is homework

How to Use This Guide

  • The capstone spans two sessions. L21 (Lesson 8.1, The Brief & the Plan) is taught in class during the Session 7 capstone kickoff (42 minutes near the end of Session 7; the Module 7 guide points here). Learners leave with a brief, a moodboard, and a plan started. Between sessions they finish the plan and build most of the piece at home (L22). Session 8 is a quick plan review, a studio block for finishing and getting help, case-study assembly, presentations with structured peer critique, and the course close.
  • Say this plainly, twice (at the Session 7 kickoff and in a midweek message): "Session 8 is not enough time to build a capstone from scratch. Arrive with Pass 1 and Pass 2 done and Pass 3 under way, about 70 to 80% of the piece. The studio block is for finishing, fixing, and getting help. Capture a screenshot at the end of every pass: you need them for the case study."
  • Teal = hands-on, amber = watch-outs. In this module the risks are a brief nobody can check, over-scoping, polishing one corner while the rest is rough, and file chaos under pressure.
  • One rubric for everything. The kit's final-assessment.html scores the capstone on 12 rows (1–3 each, max 36). Rows 2–5 are the lesson's own fresh-eyes checks, rows 6–7 and 8–11 are the L22 and L23 checklists. Use the same row numbers and names in check-ins, on the reviewers' rubric cards, and in written feedback, so learners hear one language all the way through.
  • Quiz answers below match the workbook's Self-checks and the Answer Key. The L21 quiz is homework after the kickoff; L22's is homework during the build; L23's is done after the session.
The #1 risk of Module 8: a capstone that can never be "done"

Learners at this level can execute almost anything, so the failure is rarely a skill; it is a brief with vague adjectives ("make it pop") and no checkable success criteria, which turns the build into endless fiddling and the presentation into an apology. Hold the line at the kickoff: "If you can't check it, it isn't in the brief." The second risk is generative steps. Generative Fill and Expand need internet, an Adobe sign-in, and credits; tell learners to finish them at home before Session 8, never to plan a piece that depends on generating in class, and to disclose any generative assistance in the case study.

Before Session 8

  • ☐ End of Session 7: run the L21 kickoff (lesson plan below). Show the four project options, hand out l21-brief-template.txt, and state the "Pass 1 and Pass 2 done" expectation.
  • ☐ Copy labs/module-8/practice/ to each machine (or a shared drive): the brief template, the case-study outline, the backup briefs and their assets, and the three mockups. Keep labs/module-8/instructor/ for yourself. As always, never hand out the course site's licensed images/ folder.
  • ☐ Midweek (optional but powerful): ask learners to email the brief's objective and success criteria. Reply with one sentence: a green light, a scope trim ("One hero and two sizes, not six"), or the one criterion that is not yet checkable.
  • ☐ What learners bring: the layered master and all incremental versions, the brief and moodboard, the end-of-pass screenshots and a true "before," their own photos and any licensed stock, and fonts they are licensed to use. Details in labs/module-8/README.md.
  • ☐ Version and sign-in check: anyone who used Generative Fill or Expand should have finished those steps at home. Confirm the projector machine opens JPG, PNG, and PDF, and that learners know where their Content Credentials option is (Export As or the Content Credentials panel) if they want it.
  • ☐ Room prep: a shared folder (or USB drive) for presentation files, a visible timer, help-flag cards in three colors, and printed rubric cards (final-assessment rows 2–5 and 8–10) for reviewers. Draft the presentation order and put it on the board; put a confident learner first.
  • ☐ Open lesson_21.html, lesson_22.html, and lesson_23.html in browser tabs for the before/after sliders and the checklists.

The Brief & the Plan (Lesson 8.1)

When this runs

Teaching sequence & live-demo script (Session 7 kickoff)
  1. A spec, not a wish (4m). Drag the L21 slider from the vague sticky notes ("make it pop," "you'll know it when you see it") to the structured brief. Say: "A brief is the agreement between the vision and the work, with a client or just with your future self at 2 a.m. wondering if you are done. Its job is to make success checkable. 'Make it modern' cannot be checked. 'A 3000×2000 hero image for a coffee brand's homepage, warm and editorial, readable at a glance on mobile, delivered as web + print by Friday' can." Write the mantra on the board: if you can't check it, it isn't in the brief.
  2. Warm-up: fix the sticky notes (6m). Put l21-vague-brief.txt on screen. In pairs, learners pin each phrase to a spec, a named constraint, or a reference image. Take three answers. Say: "'Soon-ish' became a date and a time. 'Our colors, I guess?' became two hex values. 'Young people should like it' became a named group, a device, and one second of attention." Your answer is in labs/module-8/instructor/l21-vague-brief-fixed.txt; accept any version where every line is checkable.
  3. Anatomy of a brief (5m). The six parts: objective (the one sentence on what this must achieve and why), audience (who, their context, what should make them stop), deliverables + specs (exact outputs, sizes, formats, where they will live), constraints (brand rules, colors, fonts, budget, and the deadline), success criteria (the checkable tests that mean "done and good"), references / moodboard. Say: "Keep it to one page. A page forces the clarity that three pages let you avoid." Then the moodboard: 6–12 references for tone, color, type, and composition, each with a note on why it is there. Say: "It aligns you, and a client, while change is still cheap. Later it is the yardstick you grade your drafts against."
  4. The plan (4m). Show Figure 3: assets & cutouts (Module 1) → color base (Module 2) → subject retouch (Module 3) → composite (Module 4) → type & system (Module 5) → automate & export (Module 6, maybe 7) → present (8.3). Say: "Not every project needs every stage, but naming them turns a blank canvas into a checklist. The commonest capstone failure is over-scoping. A tightly executed simple idea beats an ambitious half-finished one every time. Stretch goals are fine if you can cut them without breaking the deliverable."
  5. Pick your project (3m). The four options: key art poster (a composited hero with title treatment; Modules 1, 3, 4, 5), product campaign (a hero plus a sized social set from one master; Modules 2, 4, 5, 6), editorial portrait (a retouched, graded portrait with layout and type; Modules 2, 3, 5), or your own. Say: "No client? Invent one: a fictional brand, a favorite band's next album, a film that doesn't exist. Just hold yourself to the same specificity a client would demand." Point hesitant learners to l21-backup-briefs.txt.
  6. Step 1: write the brief (15m). Everyone fills l21-brief-template.txt (or the workbook page). Circulate. At the 10-minute mark, say: "Now write the success criteria as a checklist you could hand to someone else to grade." In the last 2 minutes, learners swap and read each other's success criteria aloud: any criterion the partner cannot check gets rewritten.
  7. Steps 2–3 started (6m). Create the moodboard file (a contact-sheet PSD or a simple grid is fine) and drop in the first two references with one-line annotations; list the stages and the module skill each uses; note assets to source or shoot. Say: "Anything you did not shoot: check the license and the credit terms now, not the night before."
  8. Homework and journal (2m). Finish the brief, the moodboard (6–12 annotated references), and the plan with two or three milestones and a finish date; have someone else read the success criteria; gather source assets. Then build: Pass 1 and Pass 2 done, Pass 3 under way, with a screenshot at the end of each pass and a true "before" saved. Journal prompt: the capstone's one-sentence objective.
Lab: the brief, moodboard & plan (kickoff), then the Session 8 plan review (15 min)

Kickoff: l21-vague-brief.txt warm-up, then the learner's own brief on l21-brief-template.txt or the workbook. Details in labs/module-8/README.md.

Session 8 review, 0:00–0:15. Welcome (2). In pairs (4 minutes each), the partner reads the brief's success criteria and tries to grade the current file against them, criterion by criterion: met, not yet, or "I can't check this one." Learners then write their studio goal in the workbook (the one or two things they will finish today, and what they need help with) (3). Put the day's timeline on the board (2).

Triage as you walk: red = no brief, or less than Pass 1 built; yellow = built but stuck on a specific skill; green = in Pass 3, finishing. Write initials in three columns; this list drives the first circulation round.

Definition of done: the lesson's checklist: a one-page brief with all six parts; every vague term pinned to a spec or reference; success criteria written as a checkable list; an annotated moodboard of 6–12 references; a staged plan mapping skills, assets, and milestones. This is final-assessment row 1 · Brief & plan.

Mistakes to pre-empt
  • Adjectives left floating. "Bold, clean, modern" with no reference image. Ask: "Show me the picture that means 'bold' to you."
  • Success criteria that only the author can judge ("it feels premium"). Rewrite as something a stranger could tick: "product name readable on a phone-sized thumbnail."
  • Scope for a series. Six campaign sizes, a poster, and an animation. Scale to one deliverable plus cuttable stretch goals; the lesson's "Now You" and L22's stretch list are where extras belong.
  • A moodboard as a copy list, or a pile of 40 images. 6–12, each annotated with the one thing being taken from it.
  • Unlicensed assets. Stock grabbed from a search. The plan step asks for license and credit terms checked for anything they did not shoot.
  • A plan with no dates. Two or three milestones and a realistic finish date make it possible to cut stretch goals early.
Check for understanding: self-check + journal

Answers: Q1 C) objective, audience, deliverables + specs, constraints, success criteria, references/moodboard; Q2 A) pin it to a reference image, a measurable spec, or a named constraint; Q3 D) 6–12 references, each annotated with the one thing you take from it; Q4 B) "The product name is readable on a phone-sized thumbnail"; Q5 A) it can be cut without breaking the deliverable; Q6 D) over-scoping. Full set in the Answer Key.

Journal (homework): Write your capstone's one-sentence objective. If you can't say what success looks like in a sentence, the idea isn't ready; keep refining until you can.

Differentiation
  • Confident learners: the lesson's "Going further": write the brief as if a real client sent it, then a second version as their own artist statement, and compare the success criteria.
  • Hesitant learners: hand them a backup brief (l21-backup-briefs.txt: "The Salt Road" key art poster or the Halden Cold Brew campaign). The six parts are already written; their job is the moodboard, the plan, and one success criterion of their own.

Studio Session: Build It (Lesson 8.2)

homework + 70 min studio
Objectives
  • Execute a project in passes: block-in, refine, polish
  • Keep ruthless layer hygiene: groups, names, color labels
  • Stay non-destructive with Smart Objects and adjustment layers throughout
  • Version and save so you can always step back safely
  • Use fresh-eyes tricks to judge your own work honestly
Timing (0:15–1:25)
5mStudio launch at the projector: passes, hygiene, fresh eyes, help flags, timed cues
15mRound 1: reds first, then yellows
20mRound 2 check-ins; 0:45 whole-room cue: fresh-eyes checks
20mRound 2 continues; 1:05 whole-room cue: polish pass only, no new elements
10mThe finish gate: tick success criteria, save the master, export at spec, hero to the shared folder
Teaching sequence & studio script
  1. Work in passes (launch, 2m). Drag the L22 slider: forty layers named "Layer 12 copy" against tidy groups. Say: "Same artwork; only one is still editable next week when the client asks for 'one small change.' Professionals work the whole image in passes: block-in gets every element placed roughly right, refine fixes selections, light, and color across the frame, polish adds the small, high-value details last. The image is done when a pass changes almost nothing, not when you run out of things you could do."
  2. Hygiene, shown once (launch, 1m). Groups by role, top-to-bottom in render order (Grade · Type & layout · Subject · Atmosphere & light · Background); Ctrl/⌘ + G to group, double-click to name, right-click the eye icon to color-label. Say: "Every adjustment a live layer, every scaled element a Smart Object, every filter a Smart Filter. Save capstone_v01, v02 at each pass, and keep a flattened reference JPG of milestones."
  3. Help flags and cues (launch, 2m). A card on each monitor: green = working, yellow = a question when you are free, red = blocked for five minutes. Announce the cues now: 0:45 fresh eyes, 1:05 polish only, 1:15 the finish gate.
  4. Round 1, 0:20–0:35: reds first. Three minutes max each. Goal: a finishable target for today. Say: "Which success criteria can we meet by 1:15? Everything else becomes a stretch goal." Then yellows.
  5. Round 2, 0:35–1:15: everyone. Two to three minutes per learner, the same four prompts: "Show me your brief's success criteria." · "Which pass are you in, and is it done across the whole frame?" · "What is your next move?" · file health: groups named and labeled, adjustments live, a version saved this session? Keep your hands off the mouse. If three or more learners are stuck on the same skill, run a 5-minute clinic at the projector for those learners only.
  6. 0:45 whole-room cue: fresh eyes. Say: "Everyone, four checks. View ▸ Flip Horizontal: it flips only the view; in older versions it is Image ▸ Image Rotation ▸ Flip Canvas Horizontal, so flip back after. Zoom to fit and squint: does the focal point survive at thumbnail size? Add a Black & White adjustment layer at the very top of the stack, look, then toggle it off. And tonight, walk away and look again tomorrow." Those are final-assessment rows 3, 4, and 5.
  7. 1:05 whole-room cue: polish only. Say: "No new elements now. Unifying grade on top, final details, tighten any mask at 100%, name anything unnamed. Save a version."
  8. 1:15–1:25 the finish gate. Say: "Open your brief's success criteria and tick each one on the artwork. Grade against the brief, not your mood." Save the layered master; export every deliverable at spec; export a hero JPG in sRGB named firstname-hero.jpg to the shared folder; make sure the end-of-pass screenshots and the true "before" are in one folder. Track it: tick each name when their file lands, and chase anyone without a tick at 1:22.
Lab: finish the capstone (studio block)

The learner's own capstone file, built at home through Pass 1 and Pass 2. Learners who arrive behind work from a backup brief: the key art pair (l21-backup-keyart-plate.jpg + l21-backup-keyart-figure.jpg) or the campaign set (l21-backup-product.jpg + l21-backup-product-surface.jpg + l21-backup-brand-logo.png). Details in labs/module-8/README.md.

Circulate for: a pass finished across the frame before the next begins; groups named and color-labeled; adjustments and filters still live; more than one version on disk; a Black & White check layer left switched on by accident.

Definition of done: the lesson's checklist: built in passes, whole-image at each level; grouped, named, color-labeled, non-destructive layer stack; incremental versions saved along the way; fresh-eyes checks (flip, squint, desaturate, walk away) done; every brief success criterion met and ticked; layered master saved and deliverables exported at spec. These are final-assessment rows 2–7.

Mistakes to pre-empt
  • One perfect corner. Hours on an eye at 300% while the background is still a placeholder. Ask "Which pass is the whole frame in?" and zoom them out.
  • "Layer 12 copy 3." Make naming and grouping a condition of your check-in: you help once the stack is named.
  • Baked effects under pressure. A filter run on the pixel layer, or an adjustment applied from Image ▸ Adjustments. Ask them to rebuild the one key edit as a Smart Filter or an adjustment layer.
  • The check layer left on. A Black & White layer still visible at export gives a gray deliverable. It is a temporary layer; toggle it off.
  • Huge, slow file. The lesson's mid-project rescues: move heavy, finished elements into linked Smart Objects (Layer ▸ Smart Objects ▸ Convert to Linked), rasterize truly final elements, Edit ▸ Purge ▸ Histories, sensible working resolution. Save a full layered version first. An embedded Smart Object tidies the stack but still stores every pixel, so only linked ones shrink the file.
  • Fiddling past done. Moving things a pixel back and forth. Say: "You're past done: save it and present it."
  • New generative experiments in class. Credits and network are not guaranteed; steer to finishing with what is already in the file.
Check for understanding: self-check + journal

Answers: Q1 B) raise the whole image a level at a time: block-in, refine, polish, each across the frame; Q2 D) save incrementally at each pass and keep a flattened reference JPG of milestones; Q3 A) live adjustment layers, Smart Objects, Smart Filters, grouped by role and color-labeled; Q4 C) it flips only the view, so composition and balance errors leap out; Q5 B) a temporary Black & White adjustment layer at the very top, toggled off when done; Q6 D) when a fresh-eyes pass changes almost nothing and every success criterion is met. Full set in the Answer Key.

Journal (homework): Which pass was hardest to leave "good enough" and move on from? Learning when to stop refining a corner is a professional skill worth naming.

Differentiation
  • Ahead of schedule: the lesson's stretch list: an alternate grade or a day/night variant from the same master (Modules 4.3, 5.2); spin the hero into a sized campaign set (Module 5.3) or a short animated version (Module 7.2); record the repetitive export as an Action or data set (Module 6).
  • Behind: agree a "minimum presentable" version: the focal element through Pass 2, one unifying grade, clean edges at 100%, and the success criteria it can meet today. Unfinished side elements get hidden, not rushed; the missed criteria go into the case study's reflection.

Teaching sequence & live-demo script
  1. The work plus its story (4m). Drag the L23 slider from "image only" to "case study." Say: "A portfolio full of pretty pictures says 'I can operate Photoshop.' A portfolio of case studies says 'I can solve problems.' Every real job is a problem, not a picture." Write the arc: Challenge → Approach → Process → Result → Reflection. Say: "A case study is a highlight reel, not a tutorial. Pick the three or four moments that show judgment: the tricky mask, the grade choice, the composition call."
  2. Step 1: assemble (8m). Demo on l23-mockup-wall.jpg, using the Module 5.2 recipe: draw a rectangle over the gray opening, Convert to Smart Object, double-click its thumbnail, place the hero in the tab that opens, save and close. Say: "On a real surface you would Warp or Perspective Warp the Smart Object to fit, and add the surface's shadows on Multiply and highlights on Screen above it, clipped. To swap art later, Layer ▸ Smart Objects ▸ Replace Contents; the treatment stays." The phone mockup is rotated, so its post needs a Free Transform rotate as well. Learners export firstname-context.jpg and firstname-before-after.jpg to the shared folder. Say: "Keep the before/after honest: the real starting point and the real steps."
  3. Step 2: write the story (10m). Workbook or l23-case-study-outline.txt: the five beats, a few sentences each, then the 30-second spoken version. Say: "Lead with the outcome or the idea, not the tools. 'A warm, editorial key art that reads on mobile' beats 'I used Curves and Generative Fill.' If any part used Generative Fill or Expand, say so in the process beat." While they write, load the context and before/after files on the projector in presentation order.
  4. Critique norms (3m). On the board: critique the work, not the person · point at the pixels · judge against the presenter's brief, not your taste · the presenter listens and says thank you, no defending · kind is not the same as vague. Hand out the rubric cards: final-assessment rows 2–5 (meets the brief, composition & balance, focal point & read, tonal structure) and 8–10 (hero & context, the story, honest process).
Presentation & structured peer critique protocol (about 4 min per learner)
  1. Present (90 s). The hero in context, the brief in a line, the before/after, and the 30-second story: challenge, approach, result.
  2. Squint test (15 s). The whole room squints at the projector. You ask: "What do you see first?" Two or three people call it out. Does it match the brief's focal point?
  3. Two reviewers (90 s). Assigned in advance: the next two people in the order. Each starts from the presenter's brief ("Which success criterion does it meet most clearly?"), then picks one rubric row and gives one glow (what works, specifically, pointing at the pixels) and one grow ("I wonder if…" or "What if you tried…").
  4. Presenter notes (15 s). The presenter writes the grow in the workbook's critique box and says thank you. You add at most one sentence, only if the reviewers missed something important. About 30 seconds of changeover completes the 4 minutes.

Group size: 50 minutes fits 12 learners at about 4 minutes each. For 13 or more, split into pods of 4 to 5 around a laptop with printed rubric cards and a pod timekeeper; run the same protocol in parallel for 35 minutes while you float, then each pod nominates one piece for a 15-minute whole-room showcase. For groups under 7, give each presenter 6 to 7 minutes and let everyone review.

Definition of done (homework, within one week): the lesson's checklist: a big, clean hero shot leading the piece; the five story beats written tight and outcome-first; honest before/after and 3–5 progress shots; deliverables shown in context via mockups; laid out with your type system, exported, and published; any generative-AI assistance disclosed honestly. Steps 3–4 (lay it out; export web images via File ▸ Export ▸ Export As and a PDF via File ▸ Save a Copy ▸ Photoshop PDF, or File ▸ Export ▸ Artboards to PDF) happen at home. These are final-assessment rows 8–11; the presentation is row 12.

Mistakes to pre-empt
  • Apologizing before showing ("It's not finished, I ran out of time…"). Coach it out during Step 2: start with the challenge, not the excuse. Missed criteria belong in the reflection beat.
  • A tool list instead of a story. "First I used Select Subject, then Curves…" Redirect to outcome-first and to three or four decisions.
  • Art pasted flat over the mockup. No Smart Object, so no warp and no shadow; it looks stuck on. Rebuild inside the mockup's Smart Object.
  • A fabricated "before." A deliberately worsened starting image. It costs all credibility; use the real one.
  • Reviewers who only say "I love it" or give taste ("I'd have made it blue"). Ask: "Which rubric row, what exactly on screen, and what does the brief say?"
  • Undisclosed generative content. Ask presenters to name it in the process beat; Content Credentials can be attached on export.
Course close & exit ticket (8 min, 2:52–3:00)
  1. What you have learned (2m). Read the lesson's arc: advanced selection and masking → color and tone, deep → high-end retouching → commercial compositing → design systems and type → generative and automation → multi-shot techniques → the capstone. Say: "You arrived able to operate Photoshop. You leave able to work like a professional, and with the judgment to know which tool a problem needs and when to stop."
  2. Keep growing (2m). Build a portfolio of case studies; practice one weak spot deliberately; study work you admire and name why it works; seek critique; specialize or broaden. Say: "Your next project: slightly beyond comfortable, briefed the way you learned in 8.1, finished and presented the way you learned in 8.3."
  3. Exit ticket (2m). On a card: "One thing I can do now that I couldn't at the start of this course," and the date they will publish the case study.
  4. Final journal and celebration (2m). Assign the final prompt to finish at home. Thank them; finishing an entire course of deliberate practice is rare. Applause for every piece.

Differentiation
  • Confident presenters: the lesson's "Now You": publish the case study publicly, turn two or three earlier projects into mini case studies, and (going further) ask someone in the field for honest feedback, then revise.
  • Anxious presenters: let them present from their seat, or read the 30-second version from the workbook while you drive the screen. Seat them mid-order, never first or last.

If the Room Arrives Behind

Plan B for learners who did not build at home

Some learners will arrive with a brief but little built, or with nothing. Do not let them derail the session. Scope to one deliverable and three success criteria. Offer a ready-made brief from l21-backup-briefs.txt: "The Salt Road" key art (place l21-backup-keyart-figure.jpg on l21-backup-keyart-plate.jpg: cutout, scale to the horizon, light from the left, contact and cast shadows) or the Halden Cold Brew hero (l21-backup-product.jpg on l21-backup-product-surface.jpg with l21-backup-brand-logo.png). They can complete within the one-week case-study window. If more than half the room is behind, shorten each presentation to 3 minutes and give the saved time to the studio block.
