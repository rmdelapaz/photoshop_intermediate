L21 · The Brief & the Plan (8.1)

Q1
Which list is the six parts of a one-page brief?
C) Objective, audience, deliverables + specs, constraints, success criteria, references/moodboard
Why: Those are the lesson's six parts. A is a layout's contents, B is tooling (downstream of the brief), and D mixes a constraint (budget, deadline) with document settings that belong under deliverables + specs.
Q2
A client writes "make it modern." What does the lesson say to do with it?
A) Pin it to something concrete: a reference image, a measurable spec, or a named constraint
Why: Specificity is the whole game: every vague adjective gets pinned. Adjectives are allowed; unpinned ones are not, because "modern" cannot be checked. A synonym is just as vague, and "you'll know it when you see it" is the infinite-revision trap.
Q3
How should the moodboard be built?
D) 6–12 references for tone, color, type, and composition, each annotated with the one thing you are taking from it
Why: Every tile earns its place with a reason, so the board guides decisions instead of decorating. One reference invites copying; an unlimited pile decides nothing; technique screenshots are the plan, not the look.
Q4
Which success criterion is written the way the lesson asks?
B) "The product name is readable on a phone-sized thumbnail."
Why: Success criteria are a checklist you could hand to someone else to grade. Anyone can test B by shrinking the image. "Feels premium," "as good as the references," and "the client will love it" can only be judged by the author or after the fact.
Q5
What makes a stretch goal safe to include in your plan?
A) It can be cut without breaking the deliverable
Why: "Scope to finish; make stretch goals cuttable." The milestones tell you whether you are on track, so you can cut stretch goals early. A stretch goal scheduled first, or one the piece depends on, is not a stretch goal.
Q6
According to the lesson, what is the commonest capstone failure?
D) Over-scoping: an ambitious idea that ends half-finished
Why: "A tightly executed simple idea beats an ambitious half-finished one every time." A simple idea is the recommended choice, and writing the brief first is the point of the lesson.
Reference: a solid brief and plan (rubric row 1)

Looks like: one page; all six parts; an objective in one sentence; an audience with a context and a moment of attention ("commuters, on a phone, for about one second"); deliverables with exact sizes, formats, color, and where they live, plus the layered master; constraints with hex values, a type family, and a date; five or so success criteria anyone could tick; 6–12 references each annotated with one takeaway; a staged plan (Figure 3's stages, irrelevant ones crossed out) with module skills, assets to source or shoot with licenses checked, two or three milestones, a finish date, and cuttable stretch goals. The partner in the Session 8 review can grade the current file against every criterion. labs/module-8/instructor/l21-vague-brief-fixed.txt shows one good answer to the warm-up.

Common weak briefs: Floating adjectives ("bold, clean, modern") with no reference: ask for the picture that means "bold." Author-only criteria ("feels premium"): rewrite as a test. Scope for a series (a poster, six sizes, and an animation): one deliverable plus cuttable stretch goals. A moodboard with no notes: the board decorates instead of deciding. No dates: nothing tells them when to cut. Unchecked stock: confirm the license and credit terms now.

Brief-specific checks (quoted in the final assessment): Key art poster: the hero sits at the right scale for the horizon, with matching light direction, contact and cast shadows, and atmosphere; one unifying grade; the title set from a type system and readable at thumbnail size; print and screen deliverables at spec. Product campaign: a clean cutout at 100%; light and shadow matched to the scene; the set built from one master (artboards, linked Smart Objects) with key content inside each safe zone; the export repeatable (an Action or data set is a bonus). Editorial portrait: skin texture kept, dodge & burn subtle, a natural finish on eyes, teeth, and hair; a deliberate grade; layout and type from a type system; the subject's consent. Your own: the learner's own success criteria govern, checked for specificity before the build.

L22 · Studio Session: Build It (8.2)

Q1
What does "work in passes" mean?
B) Raise the whole image a level at a time: block-in, then refine, then polish, each done across the frame
Why: Passes protect against two traps: sinking hours into a corner you later cut, and losing whole-image balance while zoomed to 300%. Finishing one corner at a time (A) is exactly what the lesson calls the amateur approach.
Q2
How does the lesson say to protect a bold experiment mid-project?
D) Save incrementally (capstone_v01, v02) at each pass and keep a flattened reference JPG of milestones
Why: Versions make a bold experiment "never a one-way door," and the reference JPGs let you compare where you were with where you are. History (and its snapshots) clears when the file closes; flattening destroys editability; a duplicated Background protects only one layer.
Q3
Which layer stack keeps every decision reversible to the end?
A) Adjustments as live layers, scaled elements as Smart Objects, filters as Smart Filters, grouped by role and color-labeled
Why: That is the lesson's layer hygiene. Merging per pass and filtering pixels directly bake decisions in; naming at the end means navigating "Layer 12 copy 3" for the whole build.
Q4
What does View ▸ Flip Horizontal do, and why use it?
C) Flips only the view, so composition and balance errors leap out in the mirror
Why: It changes the display, not the pixels, so there is nothing to undo. In older versions the equivalent is Image ▸ Image Rotation ▸ Flip Canvas Horizontal, which does flip the document, so flip back after. It has nothing to do with bleed or exports.
Q5
How do you run the desaturate check without harming the file?
B) A temporary Black & White adjustment layer at the very top of the stack, toggled off when done
Why: At the top it desaturates everything below, non-destructively, exposing tonal problems that color hides. Grayscale mode discards color; changing every layer's saturation is slow and destructive to undo; an exported JPG is a detour.
Q6
When is the capstone artwork actually finished?
D) When a fresh-eyes pass changes almost nothing and every brief success criterion is met
Why: "Done" is meeting the brief at a high polish, not exhaustion and not endless fiddling. If you are only moving things a pixel back and forth, you are past done: save it and present it.
Reference: a well-built capstone file (rubric rows 2–7)

Looks like: a layered master plus capstone_v01, v02… on disk and a flattened reference JPG per milestone; top-level groups by role in render order (Grade · Type & layout · Subject · Atmosphere & light · Background or similar), each named and color-labeled; adjustments as live layers, scaled elements as Smart Objects, filters as Smart Filters; a single unifying grade on top; every mask clean at 100%; the brief's success criteria ticked on the artwork; every deliverable exported at spec; end-of-pass screenshots and a true "before" saved for the case study.

How to check it: flip the view, squint at fit-to-screen, and add a Black & White layer at the top (rows 3–5); open the brief and tick the criteria against the image (row 2); scroll the progress shots for whole-frame passes (row 6); open the Layers panel and the folder of versions (row 7).

Common weak results: One polished corner beside rough placeholders: no passes; row 6 is 1. Flat, unnamed stack or effects baked into pixels: row 7 is 1; ask them to rebuild the one key edit non-destructively. A gray deliverable: the Black & White check layer was left on. Two focal points competing or a pasted element that goes dark in black and white: rows 4–5. A criterion quietly dropped: row 2; it belongs in the case study's reflection, not hidden. Deliverables at the wrong size or color space: the pass rule's "exported at spec" is not met; re-export before scoring.

L23 · Present & Case Study (8.3)

Q1
What is the universal case-study arc, in order?
C) Challenge → approach → process → result → reflection
Why: Every strong portfolio piece walks a reviewer through these five beats, in order. D is the capstone's workflow, not the story told about it.
Q2
What comes first on the case-study page?
A) A big, clean hero shot of the finished piece
Why: "The finished piece, big, first. Earn the scroll." Reviewers spend seconds; the brief in a line comes next, and the reflection comes last.
Q3
Which line leads with the outcome or the idea, as the lesson asks?
D) "A warm, editorial key art that reads on mobile."
Why: The lesson's own example: it beats "I used Curves and Generative Fill." Tool lists and hours spent describe effort, not the problem solved.
Q4
Why present the final piece in a mockup?
B) Context tells the reviewer what the thing is for and makes it look shipped rather than sketched
Why: An image floating on white reads as an exercise. Mockups do not excuse weak edges, and they sit alongside the before/after, not instead of it.
Q5
You laid the case study out in one document. How do you export it as a PDF?
A) File ▸ Save a Copy ▸ Photoshop PDF
Why: The lesson's Step 4. With several artboards, File ▸ Export ▸ Artboards to PDF instead. Web-sized images come from File ▸ Export ▸ Export As. Image Processor batches files; it does not make a PDF.
Q6
Part of your piece used Generative Expand. What does the lesson say?
C) Say so in the process section; you can also attach Content Credentials on export
Why: For a portfolio, disclose briefly: reviewers want to know what you made by hand and what you directed a tool to make, and many clients now ask. Using AI well is itself a skill worth showing; hiding it costs credibility.
Reference: a presented and published case study (rubric rows 8–12)

Looks like: the hero first and big; the brief in a line; an approach sentence that leads with the idea; 3–5 progress shots (the end-of-pass screenshots) and a true before/after; two or three detail crops of craft; every deliverable in a Smart-Object mockup that says what it is for (wall, feed, sleeve); one honest reflection paragraph, including any criterion that was not met; generative assistance named in the process beat. Laid out top to bottom in the learner's Module 5 type system, exported as web images and a PDF, and published. In class: a 30-second story, the hero in context, a before/after, and one specific glow and grow given to a classmate.

Common weak results: Image only, or the hero buried under text: row 8. A tutorial ("first I used Select Subject…") or a tool list: row 9; ask for the three or four decisions. No real "before" or a fabricated one, or undisclosed generative content: row 10 is 1. Art pasted flat over a mockup: no Smart Object, so no warp or shadow. Default type, not exported, or never published: row 11. Opening with an apology: coach it into the reflection beat.

Critique quality: what good peer feedback sounds like

Strong: "Your brief says the title must read on a phone thumbnail, and it does: squinting, I still see THE SALT ROAD first (glow, row 4). I wonder if the figure's shadow could fall to the right like the poles' shadows, so the light agrees (grow, row 2)." Weak: "I love it." (not specific) · "I'd have made it blue." (taste, not the brief) · "Something feels off." (not actionable). Coach weak feedback toward a rubric row, a pointable spot on screen, and the presenter's own success criteria.
