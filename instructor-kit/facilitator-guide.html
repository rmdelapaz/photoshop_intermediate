Module 1: Advanced Selection & Masking

How to open the intermediate course and teach the three lessons that rebuild selection and masking at a professional level: luminosity masks that let the image paint its own stencil, channel masks and Blend-If for subjects that tone or color makes distinct, and a four-step pipeline for the hardest common cutout, flowing hair. Everything later in the course (grading, retouching, compositing) leans on this control.

3 lessons 3 hrs session Lessons L01–L03 Level Intermediate Maps to Session 1

How to Use This Guide

  • Same rhythm as the intro kit: Frame → Demo → Guided build → Solo variation → Check. Every lesson ends with a recipe card, a checklist, and a journal prompt; use them as your closers.
  • This room already knows Photoshop. Everyone finished the intro course (or its equivalent): layers, masks, selections, Camera Raw, Curves, Smart Objects, a basic composite. Don't re-teach "black hides, white reveals." Name it, then build on it. The lessons call this "the spiral, one turn deeper."
  • Demo live, step by step, but faster than in the intro: demo a whole step, then let the room do it. Say both shortcut forms (Ctrl/⌘, Alt/⌥): the Channels-panel modifiers are the whole game in L01.
  • Teal = hands-on, amber = watch-outs. Lesson codes are the file numbers (L01–L03); the site's own numbering (Lesson 1.1–1.3) is shown alongside.
The #1 risk of Module 1: "which panel am I in?"

This is the first time most learners live in the Channels panel. Nearly every stuck moment is a panel-state problem: Ctrl/⌘-clicking a layer thumbnail instead of the RGB channel; running Levels while the RGB composite (not the channel copy) is highlighted; returning to Layers with a single channel still active so the photo looks gray; painting with the image thumbnail selected instead of the mask. Teach the room a four-point check before any click: "Which panel? Which channel or layer is highlighted? Which thumbnail has the border? Is a selection active?" Write it on the board.

Lesson Plans

L01

Luminosity Masks: Select by Tone (Lesson 1.1)

45 min
Objectives
  • Explain a luminosity mask: a selection built from the image's own luminance
  • Load a Lights or Darks selection from the channels
  • Adjust highlights and shadows independently, edges self-feathered and invisible
  • Narrow a mask to a tonal band (just the brightest highlights) by intersecting
Timing
3mThe goal: two exposures, one frame (lesson slider)
5mMental model: the image paints the mask
4mLights, Midtones & Darks; narrowing to a band
3mLoading luminosity from Channels: the four modifiers
22mGuided build: rescue the landscape (Steps 1–4: 4 + 7 + 6 + 5)
4mNow You: start the highlight glow (finish at home)
4mSelf-check, recipe card, journal
Teaching sequence & live-demo script
  1. The goal (3m). Show the L01 slider. Say: "Expose for the sky and the land goes black; expose for the land and the sky blows out. On the left, both rescued, and no brush ever touched the mask."
  2. The image paints the mask (5m). Say: "You know the rule: white reveals, black hides. A luminosity mask says: let the picture's own brightness be that stencil. Bright pixels become white, dark pixels black. The mask is literally a grayscale copy of the photo's luminance." Two payoffs: the edges are perfect (as soft as the light in the scene, no halos, no hand-feathering) and it's self-targeting (the sky is the bright part, so a Lights mask already contains it). Show Figure 1 on the lesson page: invert Lights and you have Darks. Say: "Nothing new to believe. It's two intro ideas fused: selections become masks, and masks are grayscale."
  3. A family of masks (4m). Show Figure 2: the tonal bar with overlapping Darks, Midtones, Lights, and narrower variants. Then the key move: intersecting. Say: "Load Lights, then intersect it with Lights again. Only what both share survives, so the selection slides further into the extreme tones. That's how you target a blown sky without touching a bright shirt below it." In today's file, the pale cabin plays the bright shirt.
  4. The four modifiers (3m). Show Figure 3 and write on the board: Ctrl/⌘-click RGB = load · + Shift = add · + Alt/⌥ = subtract · + Shift + Alt/⌥ = intersect. Say: "Click the channel thumbnail in the Channels panel, not the layer thumbnail in Layers. Ctrl/⌘-clicking a layer loads its transparency, which on a photo is everything."
  5. Step 1 (4m). The landscape is already open from the setup check. Image ▸ Mode ▸ 16 Bits/Channel, then File ▸ Save As luminosity-working.psd. Read the histogram out loud: highlights piling near the right, shadows near the left. In Channels, Ctrl/⌘-click the RGB thumbnail. Say: "Marching ants appear around the brighter half. The ants only outline pixels more than half selected; the real selection fades smoothly into the darks."
  6. Step 2 (7m). With the selection active, add a Curves adjustment layer. Point at the mask: "The selection wrote the mask for us." Pull the curve down to bring the sky back. Say: "Watch the land: the darkening fades out on its own." Then the spill check: if the pale cabin or the far hills darken too, undo the layer, reload Lights, and intersect once or twice (Ctrl/⌘ + Shift + Alt/⌥-click RGB) before adding the Curves layer again. Demo one intersect so the room sees the ants shrink toward the sky.
  7. Step 3 (6m). Reload the RGB luminosity selection, then invert it (Ctrl/⌘ + Shift + I): the dark valley is selected. Add another Curves layer and lift the shadows. The path, rocks and grass appear out of the black. Say: "One S-curve would brighten the sky while it lifts the land and undo our recovery. Two masked curves move highlights and shadows in opposite directions at once. That's a range only masks can reach."
  8. Step 4 (5m). Zoom to 100% (Ctrl/⌘ + 1) along the horizon and the pine edges. Say: "Try painting a mask around those pine needles by hand. Now look: no seam, no halo." If anyone sees a glow, their masks overlap: lower one layer's opacity or re-intersect. Optional: a gentle global Curves on top for overall contrast. File ▸ Save. Toggle both Curves eyeballs for the before/after.
Lab: Rescue the Landscape, then Now You

Guided build on l01-high-contrast-landscape.jpg (or a learner's high-contrast landscape). Solo variation on l01-still-life-window-light.jpg or a learner's portrait or still life: load Lights and add a subtle glow by lifting only the brightest highlights; build a Midtones mask from saved Lights and Darks channels and add contrast to only the mids; compare a luminosity-masked sky darken with a hand-painted mask. Start the glow in class; the Midtones mask and the comparison are homework. Details in labs/module-1/README.md.

Circulate for: 16-bit in the title bar; two Curves layers whose masks look like a grayscale photo (Lights) and its negative (Darks), not solid white; no glow along the horizon or around the pines at 100%.

Definition of done: the lesson's five-item checklist: highlights recovered through a Lights (RGB) mask, shadows lifted through an inverted Darks mask, no seam or halo at the horizon at 100%, every edit a non-destructive adjustment layer with a luminosity mask, 16-bit where possible. luminosity-working.psd saved.

Mistakes to pre-empt
  • Ctrl/⌘-clicking the layer thumbnail in Layers. That loads the layer's transparency: the whole image, so the mask is solid white. Go to the Channels panel and click the RGB thumbnail.
  • Curves added with no active selection. The mask comes out all white. Undo, reload Lights, then add the layer.
  • Wrong modifier combination. Ctrl/⌘ + Shift alone adds (the selection grows); Ctrl/⌘ + Alt/⌥ alone subtracts. Intersect needs all three keys.
  • "No pixels are more than 50% selected." After heavy intersecting, Photoshop warns that the selection edges won't be visible. The selection is still there, just faint. Click OK and add the adjustment; Alt/⌥-click the mask thumbnail to see it.
  • "The ants look hard-edged, so the mask must be." Ants only outline the 50% boundary. Alt/⌥-click the mask thumbnail to show the real, soft mask (click again to return).
  • Darks reloaded from an already-darkened image. The Channels panel reads what's visible, so after the sky Curves the RGB luminosity has changed a little. For the cleanest Darks, hide the first Curves layer before you reload, then turn it back on. (Saving selections with Select ▸ Save Selection before adjusting avoids this entirely.)
  • Banding in the sky. They skipped the 16-bit step. Convert and rebuild the masks.
Check for understanding: self-check + journal

Answers: Q1 B) A selection built from the image's own brightness, saved as a mask; Q2 D) Ctrl/⌘-click the RGB thumbnail in the Channels panel; Q3 A) Undo the layer, reload Lights, and intersect once or twice (Ctrl/⌘ + Shift + Alt/⌥-click RGB) before adding the adjustment; Q4 C) Reload the RGB luminosity selection, then invert it (Ctrl/⌘ + Shift + I); Q5 B) A single curve would brighten the sky while it lifts the land; two masked curves move highlights and shadows in opposite directions at once; Q6 D) You're working in 8-bit; convert to 16-bit (Image ▸ Mode ▸ 16 Bits/Channel) before building the masks. Full set in the Answer Key.

Journal (3 min, finish at home if needed): Where have you fought a hand-painted mask that a luminosity mask would have solved instantly? Name one past edit you would redo with this technique. Many will name their intro sky rescue: that's a perfect answer.

Differentiation

Fast finishers: build the Midtones mask now: save Lights and Darks as channels with Select ▸ Save Selection, then Select ▸ All and Ctrl/⌘ + Alt/⌥-click each saved channel to subtract it. Ask why Lights minus Darks is not midtones (it narrows to the brightest tones, like a second intersect). Then the lesson's "Going further": record an Action that loads Lights, Darks and Midtones as saved channels in one click.

Struggling learners: Steps 1–3 only, no intersecting. One Lights curve down, one Darks curve up is the whole idea. Put the four-modifier line from the board on a sticky note next to their keyboard.

Channel Masking & Blend-If (Lesson 1.2)

48 min
Objectives
  • Read the Red, Green & Blue channels as ready-made grayscale masks
  • Turn the highest-contrast channel into a selection (duplicate, push with Levels)
  • Use Blend-If to drop out a layer's lights or darks instantly
  • Pick the right technique for the subject: luminosity, channel, Blend-If, or a blend of all three

Teaching sequence & live-demo script
  1. The goal (3m). Show the L02 slider. Say: "Thousands of twigs. Nobody paints that, and Select Subject chokes on it. But against a bright sky, one channel already separates them almost perfectly. Every twig came along because the mask was the Blue channel."
  2. Three masks you already have (4m). Say: "Every RGB image is secretly three grayscale images stacked together. Each one is a potential mask. You're not making a mask from scratch; you're shopping among three for the one with the most separation." Walk the lesson's pipeline (Figure 1): find the highest-contrast channel → duplicate it → Levels to near black and white → Ctrl/⌘-click to load → apply as a layer mask. Say: "The whole skill is choosing the channel. The rest is two clicks and a Levels push."
  3. Reading the channels (4m). Open l02-bare-tree-sky.jpg and click Red, Green, Blue in the Channels panel while the room watches. Say: "Skies are blue, so in the Blue channel the sky is bright and the tree is dark. In Red, the top of the sky goes nearly as dark as the branches." Mention the lesson's aside: for skin, Blue is where blemishes stand out most. Same habit, different job (Module 3 uses it).
  4. Blend-If (5m). Double-click a layer's thumbnail or the empty area beside its name (not the name itself, which renames it) to open Layer Style ▸ Blending Options. Point at Blend If: This Layer and Underlying Layer. Say: "Drag This Layer's white slider inward and the layer's brightest pixels vanish; drag the black slider and its darkest pixels drop out. Alt/⌥-drag splits a slider for a soft, feathered transition." Then the rule of thumb from "When to use which": Blend-If is instant and non-destructive but tone-global; a channel mask is editable and spatial.
  5. Step 1 (4m). Image ▸ Mode ▸ 16 Bits/Channel, Save As channel-working.psd. Everyone clicks Red, Green, Blue and picks the channel where the tree is darkest and the sky brightest. Hands up: which one? (Blue.)
  6. Step 2 (8m). Drag Blue onto the New Channel icon: Blue copy appears. With only the copy highlighted, Image ▸ Adjustments ▸ Levels (Ctrl/⌘ + L). Say: "Adjustment layers can't target a channel, so this is the menu command, on a copy we're allowed to wreck." Pull the black and white points inward until the sky is white and the tree black. Zoom into the fine twigs and back off if they start to vanish. Say: "Push until the big areas are solid. The mask only has to be perfect at the edges." Paint any stray gaps with a hard black or white brush directly on the channel (on this file, the dark hedge and field are already black).
  7. Step 3 (5m). Ctrl/⌘-click Blue copy. Say: "White loads as the selection, so right now the sky is selected." Invert (Ctrl/⌘ + Shift + I). Click the RGB channel to bring back full color, go to Layers with the image layer active (click the lock first if it's still the Background), and click Add layer mask. Place l02-new-sky.jpg (File ▸ Place Embedded) and drag it below the tree layer. Zoom to 100%: every twig is there.
  8. Step 4 (5m). File ▸ Place Embedded l02-grunge-texture.jpg on top. Open Blending Options and pull the This Layer white slider left until only the texture's darker parts remain. Alt/⌥-drag to split it for a soft blend. Say: "No mask at all, purely by tone." File ▸ Save.

Guided build on l02-bare-tree-sky.jpg (or a learner's tree, power lines, or other intricate subject against plain bright sky), with l02-new-sky.jpg behind and l02-grunge-texture.jpg blended in with Blend-If. Solo variation: Blend-If the other direction, dropping a layer's shadows (l02-fireworks-on-black.jpg over l02-new-sky.jpg or a learner's night scene); extracting an intricate subject against a busy background; inspecting a portrait's Blue channel (l03-curly-hair-portrait.jpg works). Circulate for: Levels run on Blue copy, not on Blue or RGB (the photo's color should be untouched); the RGB channel clicked before the mask is added; fine twigs intact over the new sky at 100%.

Definition of done: the lesson checklist: chose the highest-contrast channel by eye, built a black-and-white mask on a duplicate channel with Levels, fine detail survived against a new background, blended a texture with Blend-If and no mask, worked non-destructively in 16-bit. channel-working.psd saved.

Mistakes to pre-empt
  • Levels on the real channel or the composite. If the photo suddenly turns yellow or the whole image goes contrasty, the original Blue (or RGB) was highlighted. Undo; click only Blue copy.
  • Looking for an adjustment layer. They can't target a channel. Image ▸ Adjustments ▸ Levels on the copy.
  • The photo looks gray back in Layers. A single channel is still active. Click RGB in the Channels panel.
  • The sky got cut out, not the tree. They forgot to invert after loading. Undo the mask, or select the mask and press Ctrl/⌘ + I to invert it.
  • Over-pushed Levels. The finest twigs snapped off (went white). Undo and push less; fix gaps by brush instead.
  • Double-clicking the layer name. That renames the layer. Double-click the thumbnail or the empty area beside the name for Blending Options.
  • This Layer vs Underlying Layer. Dragging the Underlying Layer slider forces the scene's tones through instead. For today's texture, it's This Layer, white slider.
Check for understanding: self-check + journal

Answers: Q1 A) Each of Red, Green and Blue is a full-resolution grayscale image, and a mask is just a grayscale image; Q2 C) Blue; Q3 D) The original channel is part of the photo's color; pushing it would damage the image, while the copy is a safe scratch mask; Q4 B) You over-pushed: the delicate twigs went white; back off until the big areas are solid and the twigs survive; Q5 A) White loads as the selection; invert (Ctrl/⌘ + Shift + I), click RGB, then add the layer mask on the image layer; Q6 D) Drag the This Layer white slider left (Alt/⌥-drag to split it for a soft blend). Full set in the Answer Key.

Journal (3 min): Channel masking feels like a cheat code the first time it works. What subject have you given up on cutting out before that you would now attempt? Link it to the "one edit you gave up on" from the introductions.

Differentiation

Fast finishers: the lesson's "Going further": Blend-If lives inside Layer Style, so it travels with copied layer styles and can be combined with a real mask on the same layer. Try a fast sky swap with no channel mask at all: duplicate the original tree layer above the new sky, open Blending Options, set Blend If to Blue, and drag This Layer's white slider left. Compare the twigs with the channel-mask version. Then read the FAQ on why Underlying Layer results change when layers move, and how stamping (Ctrl/⌘ + Alt/⌥ + Shift + E) locks the look.

Struggling learners: Steps 1–3 on the practice file only. The practice tree separates cleanly in Blue, so Levels needs only a modest push. Skip Step 4 in class; the recipe card covers it for homework.

  • Rescue what refinement misses with a channel mask (Lesson 1.2)
  • Kill the fringe with Decontaminate Colors and pressure-test the edge
Timing
3mThe goal: every strand survives (lesson slider)
4mMental model: a pipeline, not a tool
3mThe Select & Mask workspace, tuned for hair (Figure 2)
32mGuided build: the hard cutout (Steps 1–4: 5 + 10 + 8 + 9)

Teaching sequence & live-demo script
  1. The goal (3m). Show the L03 slider and zoom into the curls. Say: "This is the Module 1 deliverable and the true test of everything so far. Flowing, curly hair is the hardest common cutout there is."
  2. A pipeline, not a tool (4m). Say: "Beginners look for the one button that cuts out hair. There isn't one." Write the four steps on the board and leave them up: 1 Rough in (Select Subject: the solid mass, ignore the wisps) → 2 Refine (Refine Edge Brush on the hairline) → 3 Rescue (a channel mask painted into the layer mask) → 4 Decontaminate (the old background's color lets go). Say: "No single step is hard. The skill is the order, and not expecting step one to do step three's job."
  3. The workspace (3m). Show Figure 2. For hair, three things matter: the Refine Edge Brush on the hairline; Smooth and Feather kept low with Shift Edge nudged slightly negative; Output To: New Layer with Layer Mask. Decontaminate Colors is ticked only on the final pass.
  4. Step 1 (5m). Open l03-curly-hair-portrait.jpg, Image ▸ Mode ▸ 16 Bits/Channel, Save As cutout-working.psd. Duplicate the layer (Ctrl/⌘ + J) so the original stays intact beneath, and hide the original now. Select ▸ Subject. Tidy obvious misses with Quick Selection (paint to add, Alt/⌥ to subtract). Say: "It nails the face and the solid hair and gives up on the wisps. Expected. Don't chase strands yet." (On this illustrated file Select Subject may wobble; the Object Selection tool with a box around the head and shoulders is the fallback.)
  5. Step 2 (10m). Select ▸ Select and Mask (Ctrl/⌘ + Alt/⌥ + R). View: On Layers or the red Overlay. Under Edge Detection, tick Smart Radius and raise Radius a few pixels. Say: "That makes Photoshop search a wider band for strands." Try Refine Hair in the options bar for a first pass. Then the Refine Edge Brush along the curly outline, never across the solid hair. Deliberately drag it across the middle of the hair once so the room sees it go translucent and mushy, then undo. Keep Smooth and Feather low, Shift Edge slightly negative.
  6. Step 3 (8m). Output To: New Layer with Layer Mask, Decontaminate off, OK. Inspect at 100%: some fine flyaways are still missing. Now Lesson 1.2 returns: in Channels, find the channel with the most hair-vs-background contrast (on this teal backdrop, Blue or Green; Red is the weakest), duplicate it, push it with Image ▸ Adjustments ▸ Levels. Ctrl/⌘-click it and invert so the hair is selected (the backdrop loads first). Click RGB, then click the new layer's mask thumbnail and paint white with a soft, low-opacity brush along the hairline. Say: "The selection limits the paint to the strands. We're painting lost detail back." Ctrl/⌘ + D to deselect when done.

Mistakes to pre-empt
  • Original still visible underneath. The cutout looks flawless because the old background shows through the transparent edges. Hide the bottom layer before judging anything.
  • Refine Edge Brush across solid hair. Translucent, mushy patches. Undo or paint with the Brush tool in Select and Mask to restore, and stay on the boundary.
  • Smooth or Feather cranked up. Smooth erases curls into a helmet; Feather blurs the whole edge. Keep both low.
  • Decontaminate ticked on the first pass. It forces a new layer and hides the previous one, and the rescue then works on the wrong layer. Leave it for the final pass.
  • Painting with no selection, or on the image thumbnail. White everywhere on the mask brings the teal back; white on the image paints the photo. Check the thumbnail border and the ants.
  • Rescue channel not inverted. The backdrop is selected, so painting white brings back background, not strands. Invert before painting.
  • Test color layer on top. It covers the subject. The test layer goes below the cutout.
  • "Where did my layer go?" Decontaminate's output hid the previous layer. Nothing is lost: show its eyeball.

Teaching sequence & live-demo script
  1. The goal (2m). Drag the L04 slider slowly across the banded sky. Say: "Same adjustment on both sides. The only difference is the container. This is the least glamorous lesson in the course and quietly the most important: get the pipeline right and every grade after it is trustworthy."

Say: "8-bit gives you 256 steps per channel; 16-bit gives you over 32,000. And a number like R 220 means nothing until you know the container: 220 in sRGB is a different real-world color from 220 in Adobe RGB. Color management is just keeping track of the container." Show Figure 1: capture wide, edit wide and deep, convert down to the destination at the very end.
  3. Which working space? (3m). Walk the three-row table: sRGB for web, social, email ("assume the internet is sRGB"); Adobe RGB for print (it holds cyans and greens sRGB clips); ProPhoto for heavy RAW grading and archival masters, always with 16-bit. Say: "Bigger isn't automatically better. ProPhoto in 8-bit bands badly, and much of it is imaginary color no device shows."
  4. Convert vs. Assign (3m). Open l04-lagoon-adobergb.jpg (answer the mismatch dialog with Use the embedded profile). Then open l04-lagoon-untagged.jpg: same numbers, no label, and the water and parrot look duller. Say: "Identical pixel values. The only thing missing is the container label, so Photoshop reads them as the wrong space." On the untagged copy, Edit ▸ Assign Profile and pick Adobe RGB (1998): it snaps to match. Say: "That's the one honest use of Assign: labeling a file whose real space you know. At the end of a job you never relabel; you Convert, which remaps the numbers so the color keeps its appearance."
  5. Color Settings as a diagram (3m). Show Figure 3, then open Edit ▸ Color Settings live. Point at the four decisions: RGB working space, CMYK (your press profile or the default), Preserve Embedded Profiles, and the mismatch check boxes (Ask When Opening). Mistakes to pre-empt
  • All the Color Balance moves on Midtones. That's the default radio button, and it tints the skin. Switch Tone to Shadows, then Highlights.
  • Sickly skin. The split is reaching the mids. Ease the amount, keep it in the extremes, and mask the grade off the face if needed.
  • Matching color before tone. The channels never settle because the exposures differ. RGB curve first.
  • Sampling different things. A cheek in one and a backdrop in the other gives nonsense numbers. Comparable points only: the gray card patches are the safest.

Consent, respect, and how we talk about faces

Portrait retouching is personal, and at this level it gets powerful enough to change how someone looks. Set three ground rules in the warm-up and hold them all session: 1) Only edit portraits of yourself, of people who agreed to be edited and shown in class, or free-license portraits whose license allows it. 2) Nobody's portrait goes on the projector without their say-so. 3) In critique, talk about the edit, never the person's looks: "the dodge on the cheekbone is streaky," not "their skin is bad." The lesson's own position is the course position: technique is neutral, context sets the line, keep the person recognizably themselves, and be honest with clients about what was changed.

Teaching sequence & live-demo script
  1. The goal (2m). Show the L07 slider. Say: "Look at what did not change: the texture. Tone is calm, blemishes are gone, and every pore is still there. That restraint is the whole point of frequency separation."
  2. Two frequencies (3m). Draw two sheets. Low: the slow, gradual changes (cheek to shadow, a patch of redness, an uneven tan). High: the fast, tiny changes (pores, fine hairs, the grain). Say: "A blur or a soft brush destroys both at once, which is why it looks like melted plastic. Split them, and you calm the tone on one layer and touch texture only on the other." Remind them this is the spiral from the intro course: same idea, production depth.
  3. The stack and the math (6m). Put the finished stack on the board: High Frequency (Linear Light, texture only) over Low Frequency (Normal, Gaussian Blur) over the untouched Background. Say: "Apply Image subtracts the blurred Low layer itself, so the radius matches automatically; there is no second radius to enter. What has to match is the bit depth." Write both recipes side by side: 8-bit: Blending Subtract, Scale 2, Offset 128. 16-bit: Blending Add, Invert ticked, Scale 2, Offset 0. Say: "Use the 8-bit numbers on a 16-bit file and the stack will not add back to the original. Check the title bar before you open Apply Image." Mention the High Pass shortcut only as the approximate fallback: same radius as the blur, and only approximate.
  4. Step 1: make the layers (10m). Open l07-portrait-retouch.jpg, Image ▸ Mode ▸ 16 Bits/Channel. Say: "Global corrections first, always. Frequency separation is a local cleanup, not a substitute for exposure and white balance." Duplicate the layer twice (Ctrl/⌘ + J, twice). Name the lower copy Low and the upper High. Hide High (the recipe card's tip: otherwise you can't see the blur). Select Low: Filter ▸ Blur ▸ Gaussian Blur, zoom to 100% on a cheek, raise the radius slowly. Say: "Stop the moment the pores vanish but the features stay. On this file that's around 5 or 6 pixels. Write your number down." Show High, select it, Image ▸ Apply Image: Layer Low, Channel RGB, Blending Add, Invert ticked, Scale 2, Offset 0. Read each field aloud. OK, then set the blend mode to Linear Light. Say: "Checkpoint. It looks exactly like the original again. If yours doesn't, hands up now, before anyone paints."
  5. Step 2: even the tone on Low (10m). Select Low. Healing Brush, big and soft, Sample: Current Layer; Alt/⌥-click calm skin and gently push the redness beside the nose toward its neighbors. Then show the alternative: Mixer Brush at low opacity with Sample All Layers off. Do the cheek redness and the muddy patch under one eye. Toggle High's eye off and on. Say: "Because you're only on the color layer, texture never moves. The pores are exactly as sharp as before."
  6. Step 3: blemishes on High (8m). Select High. Healing Brush (or Clone Stamp), Sample: Current Layer, Alt/⌥-click clean texture near a blemish and heal over it. Do two on the cheek. Say: "Only the texture changes; the tone underneath stays put, so there's no soft patch or color smear where the spot was." If a faint pink ghost remains, go back to Low and even it: that is the two-layer logic in action.
  7. Step 4: dial it back (5m). Select both layers, group them (Ctrl/⌘ + G), lower the group's opacity to about 80%. Zoom to 100% and pan across the face. Say: "Real pores everywhere? Good. If it looks poreless, you smoothed too hard. Letting a little of the original through is what keeps it human." File ▸ Save As → skin-retouched.psd, layers intact: Say: "Lesson 3.2 opens this exact file."
  8. Now You (5m, finish at home). Hand out l07-beauty-closeup.jpg and l07-candid-distance.jpg (or learners' own two portraits). Each learner finds the radius where the pores just vanish on both and writes both numbers in the workbook. Say: "The close shot needs a bigger radius because its pores are bigger in pixels, and it needs far more restraint."

Mistakes to pre-empt
  • Gray mess or a shifted image after Apply Image. Almost always the wrong recipe for the bit depth (the lesson FAQ's number one), then Linear Light not set, then Layer in Apply Image not set to Low. Check the title bar: 8-bit = Subtract / Scale 2 / Offset 128; 16-bit = Add / Invert / Scale 2 / Offset 0. Fastest fix: delete High, duplicate the original again, redo Apply Image.
  • High shows eyes, lips, and face shapes. The blur radius was too large, so real features moved into the texture layer. Rebuild with a smaller radius. A High layer that is almost blank means the opposite: the radius is too small and the pores stayed on Low.
  • Can't see the blur. High is still visible on top. Hide it while blurring Low, then show it for Apply Image.
  • Healing on the wrong layer, or with the wrong Sample. Heal on High with Sample: All Layers (or Current & Below) drags color onto the texture layer and leaves a smear. Mantra: tone on Low, texture on High, each with Sample: Current Layer.
  • Poreless, plastic skin. Heavy strokes on Low that flatten the face's shading, or a group left at 100%. Lower the group opacity to 70–85% and look at 100% zoom, not fit-to-screen.
  • Skipping global corrections. Trying to fix an overall color cast on Low, patch by patch. Fix exposure and white balance first (Module 2), then separate.

Teaching sequence & live-demo script
  1. The goal (2m). Show the L08 slider: a flat sphere and a sculpted one. Say: "A little burn in the core shadow and a dab of dodge on the highlight, and you get obvious volume. Even, clean skin from L07 can still look flat. This is how we give the face its third dimension back."
  2. Painting with light (3m). Say: "A camera flattens a face; our brains read depth from where light falls. Dodging lightens and pulls a plane toward the viewer. Burning darkens and pushes it back. We exaggerate the light the lens already captured." Then the professional habit: low and slow. Say: "You don't lighten a cheekbone in one stroke. Many passes, each a whisper. Streaky dodge and burn is just impatient dodge and burn."
  3. The map and the gray layer (5m). Draw the face map: dodge forehead, cheekbones, nose (bridge and tip), brow bone; burn eye sockets, sides of the nose, under the cheekbones, under the jaw. Say: "Follow the bone structure the light already implies. Don't invent a light source." Then why the gray layer: the Dodge and Burn tools edit pixels directly and are hard to undo; a 50% gray layer in Soft Light is invisible until you paint on it, and every stroke stays adjustable, reversible, and maskable. Finish with the check: Say: "Color hides uneven sculpting. A temporary Black & White layer on top shows every lump and streak."

Mistakes to pre-empt
  • The gray layer lands inside the L07 group. The top layer of the expanded group was selected, so the new layer inherits the group's opacity. Drag it above the group, or collapse the group and select it before adding the layer.
  • Solid gray over the photo. The mode stayed on Normal. Set the layer to Soft Light. (A transparent layer instead means the fill checkbox was skipped; it only appears after choosing Soft Light in the dialog.)
  • Streaks and blotches. Flow at 100% or a hard brush. Undo, set Hardness 0 and Flow 5–10%, and brush over the plane many times.
  • Painting on the photo. White and black paint appears directly on the face. Undo, select the gray layer.
  • Inventing light. Dodging the shadow side "because it looks dark," or a symmetric map on a face lit from one side. Follow the light that's already there.
  • Chalky, overdone sculpting. If you can point to it, it's too strong. Lower the layer opacity; that is the finishing move, not an admission of failure.

Teaching sequence & live-demo script
  1. The last five percent (2m). Show the L09 slider. Say: "The skin is untouched in this comparison. Only the eyes and teeth changed, and it's enough to make the face read as more awake.   3. Step 1: eyes (11m). Open portrait-sculpted.psd (or l09-portrait-sculpted.jpg). Add a Curves adjustment layer, lift the midtones a little. It only flips the view, not the pixels, so choose it again to come back. Mistakes to pre-empt
  • The whole face got brighter. The Curves mask is still white. Select the mask thumbnail and press Ctrl/⌘ + I, then paint white only on the eyes.
  • Bright yellow teeth, or blue-white teeth. Brightened before removing yellow, or the Yellows saturation and Master lightness pushed too far. Yellow out first, lift gently, stop at "faintly warm."

  • Helmet hair. Every stray cloned away, or long strokes that ignore the hair's flow. Short strokes along the strands; leave some strays.

Teaching sequence & live-demo script

Before light, before color, the element has to agree with the scene's horizon, its vanishing points, and its lens. Light can't save a giant."
  2. Everyone shares a horizon (5m). Say: "Every photograph has a horizon at the camera's eye level, whether or not you can see it. It's the height the lens was at."   3. Scale by the ground (4m). Open l10-plaza-background.jpg. Say: "For people of similar height on the same flat ground, the horizon crosses each of them at the same point on their body. This camera was at standing eye level, so the horizon cuts every person at the eyes, near or far." Point at the red-jacketed man in front and the green figure far back: both have their eyes on the horizon. Then the callout: Say: "Feet on the ground line first. The contact point fixes distance and size."
  4. Matching the lens (4m). Say: "A wide lens (say 24mm) exaggerates depth and bends verticals near the edges; a long lens (say 200mm) compresses depth and flattens features. Paste a telephoto headshot onto a wide street and it looks subtly wrong even with perspective matched." Show Figure 3: Edit ▸ Perspective Warp has Layout (draw a quad over each plane) and Warp (drag its pins). Then the warning: Say: "Warp fixes geometry, not optics. You can't add depth a long lens removed. If the lenses are wildly different, find a better source photo."

Say: "Proportional by default in current versions. Don't hold Shift: Shift now switches proportional scaling off." Mistakes to pre-empt
  • Guessing the horizon at the visible skyline. The skyline here is hills; the horizon is the eye level where the lines meet. On most street photos they differ.
  • Scaling first, placing second. The contact point decides the size. Fix the feet, then scale from the bottom-center reference point.
  • Holding Shift out of intro-course habit. In current versions that distorts the element. If a learner's figure looks stretched, this is why.
  • Warping a person. Perspective Warp is for planes (boxes, signs, buildings). A person needs correct scale and eye level, plus at most a small Distort.

Definition of done (the lesson's checklist): element re-lit to match the scene's direction, hardness, and color; tight contact shadow at the base (Multiply); cast shadow running away from the light, blurred and faded with distance; shadows tinted (not pure black) to match the scene; reflection added if the surface is glossy.

Circulate for: shadow layers sitting above the element; a cast shadow pointing right (toward the sun); one uniform Gaussian Blur on the whole cast shadow; the D&B layer not clipped (the ↳ is missing and the plaza gets painted too); pure black shadows at 100% opacity.

Mistakes to pre-empt
  • Shadow direction by guesswork. Read the scene's existing shadows first. On the plaza every lamp post and person shadow falls to the left, so yours must too.
  • Using the Drop Shadow layer style as is. It sits at a fixed offset and never lies on the ground in perspective. As a starting point only: Layer ▸ Layer Style ▸ Create Layer splits it onto its own layer, which can then be distorted, blurred and masked.
  • Too dark. Match the scene's real shadows, then usually pull back 5–10%. Real shadows carry bounced light.
  • Blurring the contact shadow like the cast shadow. The contact shadow stays tight; only the cast shadow gets progressive blur.
  • Relighting with the Dodge and Burn tools on the element's pixels. Use the clipped 50% gray Soft Light layer from Lesson 3.2 so it stays editable.
Check for understanding: self-check + journal

Differentiation

Fast finishers: the colored-source variation (let a sunset or neon color spill onto the element's lit edge; the glossy scene's magenta neon strip is a ready-made source) and the lesson's "Going further": a subtle rim light, a thin bright edge on a masked layer where the light wraps the element. Struggling learners: contact shadow first, then the cast shadow without the progressive blur (one moderate Gaussian Blur plus a gradient mask). Skip the reflection. The lesson's own FAQ backs this up: a good contact shadow sells the grounding even when the cast shadow is subtle.

Teaching sequence & live-demo script
  1. Frame: one voice, not twenty (3m). Project l13-ransom-note-before.png, then the lesson's slider. Ask: "Count the fonts. Count the alignments." Say: "Same words, completely different credibility. Say: "A style bottles the whole spec: font, size, leading, tracking, color, OpenType. And never press Faux Bold or Faux Italic: they smear a regular weight. No real bold in the family? Mistakes to pre-empt

  • Local overrides. Changing the size of styled text by hand breaks the link to the style. Clear the override (Paragraph Styles panel) and edit the style instead.
  • Points vs pixels confusion. Units & Rulers ▸ Type still on Points while the plan says px. Change the preference or use the same numbers as pt, consistently.

Teaching sequence & live-demo script
  1. The goal (1m). Show the L16 slider: cramped and signed on one side, extended and clean on the other. Say: "Generative fill did the grunt work, new sky and ground and the sign gone, so the time goes into composition instead of cloning. Fill the gaps, not the vision."

For client work, know which model you used and check the terms. And for journalism or documentary work, generative changes may be off-limits." Mistakes to pre-empt

  • Expanding with the subject visible. The model tries to continue the person at the edge. Hide the subject while expanding and removing.
  • Removing the sign but not its shadow. A shadow with nothing casting it is a bigger tell than the sign was.

  • Expecting a percent crop to give the same ratio everywhere. It trims the same share of each image. On files of different ratios the result differs a little; that's why the branch exists, and why you test on odd files.

  • Forgetting to press Stop, so browsing and fixes are recorded too. Delete the stray steps from the panel.

Teaching sequence & live-demo script
  1. The goal (3m). Show the L19 slider: one frame catches a slice, three stitched frames hold the whole vista. Say: "A single frame has three limits: it is only so wide, it only holds so much range from shadow to highlight, and only so much of it is in focus. Today we beat all three with the same trick: shoot several frames and let Photoshop combine them."
  2. Align, then blend (5m). Draw the pipeline on the board: source frames → Auto-Align → Blend → one image. Say: "Auto-Align finds common detail and warps the frames into registration. Blend then picks, pixel by pixel, the best contribution: the overlap for a pano, the well-exposed tone for HDR, the sharpest region for a stack." Then the three-column table: vary angle → wider field; vary exposure → wider range; vary focus → deeper focus. Say: "Change only the one variable the technique needs. Hold everything else still. That is why a tripod and a 2-second timer pay off, especially for HDR and stacking, where frames must register to the pixel."
  3. Three front doors (4m). Point at each menu without running it yet: File ▸ Automate ▸ Photomerge; File ▸ Automate ▸ Merge to HDR Pro; File ▸ Scripts ▸ Load Files into Stack followed by Edit ▸ Auto-Align Layers and Edit ▸ Auto-Blend Layers…. Then the warning from the lesson: Say: "Garbage frames in, garbage merge out. Too little overlap and the pano won't stitch; something moves mid-bracket and HDR shows ghosts; skip a focus distance and the stack has a soft band."
  4. Step 1: panorama (10m). File ▸ Automate ▸ Photomerge → Browse → select the four files in l19-pano/. Layout Auto; tick Blend Images Together and Content Aware Fill Transparent Areas; OK. While it runs: Say: "These frames overlap by about 35%. The dialog doesn't measure overlap for you; aim for roughly 30% when you shoot." Show the result's layer masks (one per frame): the stitch is just masks, the Module 1 idea again. Crop or accept the filled edges, then zoom to 100% on a seam and heal anything that doubled. Save as pano-working.psd (suggested name). Say: "The frames were deliberately shot with slightly different brightness and some vignetting. Blend Images Together is what hides that."
  5. Step 2: HDR (12m). File ▸ Automate ▸ Merge to HDR Pro → Browse → the three files in l19-hdr/; leave Attempt to Automatically Align Source Images on; OK. In the merge dialog, tick Remove Ghosts. Say: "A bird flew across the window between shots. Toggle Remove Ghosts and watch the three faint birds become one." Set Mode: 16 Bit and choose Local Adaptation; nudge the sliders toward believable: window detail back, room detail visible, no halos around the window frame. Say: "Keep it believable. Want every stop kept for later? Set 32 Bit instead and tone-map afterwards with Camera Raw." OK; save as hdr-working.psd. If a learner gets the Manually Set EV dialog, walk them through the README values.
  6. Step 3: focus stack (10m). File ▸ Scripts ▸ Load Files into Stack → Browse → the four files in l19-stack/; tick Attempt to Automatically Align Source Images; OK. Zoom to 100% on the ruler and the wall chart's fine print. Say: "If a patch is soft, paint the mask of the frame that had it sharp. Auto-Blend can only choose from the sharp regions you captured." Mistakes to pre-empt
  • Transparent, ragged edges on the panorama. Content Aware Fill Transparent Areas was unticked. Crop them off, or rerun with the tick. Either is fine; checking the filled edges at 100% is not optional.
  • The pano bows or bends. Wide pans on a flat projection curve straight lines. Try Cylindrical or Spherical in Photomerge's Layout list; for their own shoots, keep the horizon centered; Filter ▸ Adaptive Wide Angle straightens afterwards.
  • Three ghost birds in the window. Remove Ghosts was off. Re-open the merge and tick it; for their own brackets, the lesson's fix is prevention (shoot when nothing moves).
  • HDR result looks radioactive. Every slider pushed to the right. Ask: "Would a viewer believe this room?" Pull Detail and Vibrance back; the overcooked version is a Now You exercise, not the deliverable.
  • Auto-Blend grayed out or odd. Only one layer selected, or the layers are still the locked Background. Select all four (click the top one, Shift-click the bottom) and run it again.
  • "Panorama" chosen in Auto-Blend. That is the other Blend Method. Stack Images is the focus-stacking option.
  • A soft band across the table. On the practice set this only happens if a frame was left out (try it deliberately: drop frame 2 and watch the band appear). On real shoots: reshoot with smaller focus steps, or mask in a sharper frame by hand.

Mistakes to pre-empt

  • Changed a value but no keyframe appeared. The stopwatch was never clicked, so the change applied to the whole clip. Click the stopwatch first, then change values at new times.

Professionals work the whole image in passes: block-in gets every element placed roughly right, refine fixes selections, light, and color across the frame, polish adds the small, high-value details last. The image is done when a pass changes almost nothing, not when you run out of things you could do."
  2. Hygiene, shown once (launch, 1m). Groups by role, top-to-bottom in render order (Grade · Type & layout · Subject · Atmosphere & light · Background); Ctrl/⌘ + G to group, double-click to name, right-click the eye icon to color-label. Say: "Every adjustment a live layer, every scaled element a Smart Object, every filter a Smart Filter. Save capstone_v01, v02 at each pass, and keep a flattened reference JPG of milestones."
  3. Help flags and cues (launch, 2m). A card on each monitor: green = working, yellow = a question when you are free, red = blocked for five minutes. Announce the cues now: 0:45 fresh eyes, 1:05 polish only, 1:15 the finish gate.
  4. Round 1, 0:20–0:35: reds first. Three minutes max each. Goal: a finishable target for today. Say: "Which success criteria can we meet by 1:15? Everything else becomes a stretch goal." Then yellows.
  5. Round 2, 0:35–1:15: everyone. Two to three minutes per learner, the same four prompts: "Show me your brief's success criteria." · "Which pass are you in, and is it done across the whole frame?" · "What is your next move?" · file health: groups named and labeled, adjustments live, a version saved this session? Keep your hands off the mouse. If three or more learners are stuck on the same skill, run a 5-minute clinic at the projector for those learners only.
  6. 0:45 whole-room cue: fresh eyes. Say: "Everyone, four checks. View ▸ Flip Horizontal: it flips only the view; in older versions it is Image ▸ Image Rotation ▸ Flip Canvas Horizontal, so flip back after. Zoom to fit and squint: does the focal point survive at thumbnail size? Add a Black & White adjustment layer at the very top of the stack, look, then toggle it off. And tonight, walk away and look again tomorrow." Those are final-assessment rows 3, 4, and 5.
  7. 1:05 whole-room cue: polish only. Say: "No new elements now. Unifying grade on top, final details, tighten any mask at 100%, name anything unnamed. Save a version."
  8. 1:15–1:25 the finish gate. Say: "Open your brief's success criteria and tick each one on the artwork. Grade against the brief, not your mood." Save the layered master; export every deliverable at spec; export a hero JPG in sRGB named firstname-hero.jpg to the shared folder; make sure the end-of-pass screenshots and the true "before" are in one folder. Track it: tick each name when their file lands, and chase anyone without a tick at 1:22.

Definition of done: the lesson's checklist: built in passes, whole-image at each level; grouped, named, color-labeled, non-destructive layer stack; incremental versions saved along the way; fresh-eyes checks (flip, squint, desaturate, walk away) done; every brief success criterion met and ticked; layered master saved and deliverables exported at spec. These are final-assessment rows 2–7.

Mistakes to pre-empt
  • One perfect corner. Hours on an eye at 300% while the background is still a placeholder. Ask "Which pass is the whole frame in?" and zoom them out.
  • "Layer 12 copy 3." Make naming and grouping a condition of your check-in: you help once the stack is named.
  • Baked effects under pressure. A filter run on the pixel layer, or an adjustment applied from Image ▸ Adjustments. Ask them to rebuild the one key edit as a Smart Filter or an adjustment layer.
  • The check layer left on. A Black & White layer still visible at export gives a gray deliverable. It is a temporary layer; toggle it off.
  • Huge, slow file. The lesson's mid-project rescues: move heavy, finished elements into linked Smart Objects (Layer ▸ Smart Objects ▸ Convert to Linked), rasterize truly final elements, Edit ▸ Purge ▸ Histories, sensible working resolution. Save a full layered version first. An embedded Smart Object tidies the stack but still stores every pixel, so only linked ones shrink the file.
  • Fiddling past done. Moving things a pixel back and forth. Say: "You're past done: save it and present it."
  • New generative experiments in class. Credits and network are not guaranteed; steer to finishing with what is already in the file.

Teaching sequence & live-demo script
  1. The work plus its story (4m). Drag the L23 slider from "image only" to "case study." Say: "A portfolio full of pretty pictures says 'I can operate Photoshop.' A portfolio of case studies says 'I can solve problems.' Every real job is a problem, not a picture." Write the arc: Challenge → Approach → Process → Result → Reflection. Say: "A case study is a highlight reel, not a tutorial. Pick the three or four moments that show judgment: the tricky mask, the grade choice, the composition call."
  2. Step 1: assemble (8m). Demo on l23-mockup-wall.jpg, using the Module 5.2 recipe: draw a rectangle over the gray opening, Convert to Smart Object, double-click its thumbnail, place the hero in the tab that opens, save and close. Say: "On a real surface you would Warp or Perspective Warp the Smart Object to fit, and add the surface's shadows on Multiply and highlights on Screen above it, clipped. To swap art later, Layer ▸ Smart Objects ▸ Replace Contents; the treatment stays." The phone mockup is rotated, so its post needs a Free Transform rotate as well. Learners export firstname-context.jpg and firstname-before-after.jpg to the shared folder. Say: "Keep the before/after honest: the real starting point and the real steps."
  3. Step 2: write the story (10m). Workbook or l23-case-study-outline.txt: the five beats, a few sentences each, then the 30-second spoken version. Say: "Lead with the outcome or the idea, not the tools. 'A warm, editorial key art that reads on mobile' beats 'I used Curves and Generative Fill.' If any part used Generative Fill or Expand, say so in the process beat." While they write, load the context and before/after files on the projector in presentation order.
  4. Critique norms (3m). On the board: critique the work, not the person · point at the pixels · judge against the presenter's brief, not your taste · the presenter listens and says thank you, no defending · kind is not the same as vague. Hand out the rubric cards: final-assessment rows 2–5 (meets the brief, composition & balance, focal point & read, tonal structure) and 8–10 (hero & context, the story, honest process).
Presentation & structured peer critique protocol (about 4 min per learner)
  1. Present (90 s). The hero in context, the brief in a line, the before/after, and the 30-second story: challenge, approach, result.
  2. Squint test (15 s). The whole room squints at the projector. You ask: "What do you see first?" Two or three people call it out. Does it match the brief's focal point?
  3. Two reviewers (90 s). Assigned in advance: the next two people in the order. Each starts from the presenter's brief ("Which success criterion does it meet most clearly?"), then picks one rubric row and gives one glow (what works, specifically, pointing at the pixels) and one grow ("I wonder if…" or "What if you tried…").
  4. Presenter notes (15 s). The presenter writes the grow in the workbook's critique box and says thank you. You add at most one sentence, only if the reviewers missed something important. About 30 seconds of changeover completes the 4 minutes.

Group size: 50 minutes fits 12 learners at about 4 minutes each. For 13 or more, split into pods of 4 to 5 around a laptop with printed rubric cards and a pod timekeeper; run the same protocol in parallel for 35 minutes while you float, then each pod nominates one piece for a 15-minute whole-room showcase. For groups under 7, give each presenter 6 to 7 minutes and let everyone review.

Definition of done (homework, within one week): the lesson's checklist: a big, clean hero shot leading the piece; the five story beats written tight and outcome-first; honest before/after and 3–5 progress shots; deliverables shown in context via mockups; laid out with your type system, exported, and published; any generative-AI assistance disclosed honestly. Steps 3–4 (lay it out; export web images via File ▸ Export ▸ Export As and a PDF via File ▸ Save a Copy ▸ Photoshop PDF, or File ▸ Export ▸ Artboards to PDF) happen at home. These are final-assessment rows 8–11; the presentation is row 12.

Mistakes to pre-empt
  • Apologizing before showing ("It's not finished, I ran out of time…"). Coach it out during Step 2: start with the challenge, not the excuse. Missed criteria belong in the reflection beat.
  • A tool list instead of a story. "First I used Select Subject, then Curves…" Redirect to outcome-first and to three or four decisions.
  • Art pasted flat over the mockup. No Smart Object, so no warp and no shadow; it looks stuck on. Rebuild inside the mockup's Smart Object.
  • A fabricated "before." A deliberately worsened starting image. It costs all credibility; use the real one.
  • Reviewers who only say "I love it" or give taste ("I'd have made it blue"). Ask: "Which rubric row, what exactly on screen, and what does the brief say?"
  • Undisclosed generative content. Ask presenters to name it in the process beat; Content Credentials can be attached on export.
Course close & exit ticket (8 min, 2:52–3:00)
  1. What you have learned (2m). Read the lesson's arc: advanced selection and masking → color and tone, deep → high-end retouching → commercial compositing → design systems and type → generative and automation → multi-shot techniques → the capstone. Say: "You arrived able to operate Photoshop. You leave able to work like a professional, and with the judgment to know which tool a problem needs and when to stop."
  2. Keep growing (2m). Build a portfolio of case studies; practice one weak spot deliberately; study work you admire and name why it works; seek critique; specialize or broaden. Say: "Your next project: slightly beyond comfortable, briefed the way you learned in 8.1, finished and presented the way you learned in 8.3."
  3. Exit ticket (2m). On a card: "One thing I can do now that I couldn't at the start of this course," and the date they will publish the case study.
  4. Final journal and celebration (2m). Assign the final prompt to finish at home. Thank them; finishing an entire course of deliberate practice is rare. Applause for every piece.
