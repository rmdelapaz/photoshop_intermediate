L01

Luminosity Masks: Select by Tone (1.1)

55 min
By the end you can
  • Explain a luminosity mask: a selection built from the image's own luminance
  • Load a Lights or Darks selection from the channels
  • Adjust highlights and shadows independently, edges self-feathered and invisible
  • Narrow a mask to a tonal band (just the brightest highlights) by intersecting
Key ideas
  • The image paints the mask. Load a Lights mask and the bright pixels become white (selected, editable) while the dark pixels become black (protected). The mask is a grayscale copy of the photo's luminance.
  • The edges are perfect. The mask follows the actual tones, so its transitions are exactly as soft as the light in the scene: no halos, no hand-feathering.
  • It's self-targeting. The sky is the bright part, so a Lights mask already contains it. No painting around the horizon.
  • Invert Lights and you have Darks. Intersect Lights with itself and the selection slides into the brightest tones only.
  • Why two curves beat one: a single S-curve brightens the sky while it lifts the land. Two masked curves move highlights and shadows in opposite directions at once.
In the Channels panel, on the RGB thumbnailDoes
Ctrl/⌘-clickLoad Lights (a new selection)
Ctrl/⌘ + Shift-clickAdd to the current selection
Ctrl/⌘ + Alt/⌥-clickSubtract from the current selection
Ctrl/⌘ + Shift + Alt/⌥-clickIntersect: keep only what both share
Worked example: rescue the landscape
  1. Open a high-contrast photo (or l01-high-contrast-landscape.jpg). Image ▸ Mode ▸ 16 Bits/Channel, then Save As luminosity-working.psd.
  2. Diagnose, then load Lights. Read the histogram: highlights piling near the right, shadows near the left. In Channels, Ctrl/⌘-click the RGB thumbnail.
  3. Tame the highlights. With the selection active, add a Curves adjustment layer (the selection becomes its mask) and pull the curve down to bring the sky back. Spilling onto the land? Undo the layer, reload Lights, intersect once or twice, then add Curves again.
  4. Lift the shadows. Reload the RGB luminosity selection, invert it (Ctrl/⌘ + Shift + I), add another Curves layer, and lift the shadows.
  5. Check and finish. Zoom to 100% along the horizon: no seam, no glow. A glow means the masks overlap: reduce one layer's opacity or re-intersect. Optional gentle global Curves on top. Save the layered master.
Recipe card: Luminosity Mask
  1. Channels panel: Ctrl/⌘-click RGB = Lights selection
  2. Add a Curves layer → the selection becomes its mask
  3. Too broad? Intersect (Ctrl/⌘ + Shift + Alt/⌥-click RGB) to reach extreme tones
  4. Shadows: reload, invert (Ctrl/⌘ + Shift + I), add another Curves
  5. Check the horizon at 100% for seams; work in 16-bit

Mantra: let the image paint the mask.

L02

Channel Masking & Blend-If (1.2)

60 min
By the end you can
  • Read the Red, Green & Blue channels as ready-made grayscale masks
  • Turn the highest-contrast channel into a selection (duplicate, push with Levels)
  • Use Blend-If to drop out a layer's lights or darks instantly
  • Pick the right technique for the subject: luminosity, channel, Blend-If, or a blend of all three
Key ideas
  • Three masks you already have. Every RGB image is three grayscale images stacked: Red, Green, Blue. Each is a potential mask. You shop for the one with the most subject/background separation.
  • Skies are blue, so the Blue channel keeps a sky bright while dark branches stay dark. (For skin, Blue is where blemishes stand out most: same habit, different job.)
  • Forge, don't paint: duplicate the winning channel, then push the copy toward black and white with Image ▸ Adjustments ▸ Levels. Adjustment layers can't target a channel.
  • White loads as the selection. Against a bright sky, the sky loads first, so invert to get the subject.
  • Blend-If (Layer Style ▸ Blending Options) hides a layer's pixels by tone in real time. This Layer white slider inward drops the layer's lights; black slider inward drops its darks. Alt/⌥-drag to split a slider for a soft blend.
  • When to use which: Blend-If is instant and non-destructive but tone-global. A channel mask is editable and spatial: you can paint on it afterward.
Worked example: extract a tree
  1. Open a bare tree, power lines, or other intricate subject against plain bright sky (or l02-bare-tree-sky.jpg). 16-bit, Save As channel-working.psd.
  2. Channel-shop. In Channels, click Red, then Green, then Blue. Pick the one where the subject is darkest and the background brightest.
  3. Forge the mask. Drag the winner onto the New Channel icon. With only the copy selected, Image ▸ Adjustments ▸ Levels (Ctrl/⌘ + L): pull the black and white points inward until the sky is white and the tree black, fine twigs still intact. Paint stray gaps with a hard black or white brush on the channel.
  4. Load it and cut. Ctrl/⌘-click the copy, invert (Ctrl/⌘ + Shift + I), click RGB, go to Layers with the image layer active, and add a layer mask. Drop a new sky behind it (l02-new-sky.jpg) to check the edges.
  5. A Blend-If finish. Place a texture (l02-grunge-texture.jpg) as a new layer on top. Blending Options: pull the This Layer white slider left until only the darker parts remain; Alt/⌥-split for a soft blend. No mask at all.
Recipe card: Channel Mask
  1. Channels panel: click R / G / B, pick the highest-contrast one
  2. Duplicate it → Image ▸ Adjustments ▸ Levels to force near black & white
  3. Clean gaps with a hard brush on the channel
  4. Ctrl/⌘-click to load → invert (the white background loads first) → click RGB → add layer mask
  5. Fast alternative: Blend-If to drop lights or darks by tone (Alt/⌥-split to feather)

Mantra: shop the channels before you paint.

L03

Select & Mask Mastery: The Hard Cutout (1.3)

60 min
By the end you can
  • Run the full Select & Mask workflow, tuned for hair
  • Rebuild wispy strands with the Refine Edge Brush
  • Rescue what refinement misses with a channel mask (Lesson 1.2)
  • Kill the fringe with Decontaminate Colors and pressure-test the edge
Key ideas
  • A pipeline, not a tool. There is no one button for hair. Each step cleans up what the previous one couldn't.
  • Rough in the solid mass (Select Subject, Quick Selection) → refine the hairline (Refine Edge Brush) → rescue missing strands (channel mask painted into the layer mask) → decontaminate the edge (the old background's color lets go).
  • Refine the edge, not the middle. The Refine Edge Brush across solid hair turns it translucent and mushy.
  • Decontaminate last. It rewrites edge pixels, so it can only output to a new layer or document.
  • The standard: fine strands kept, no colored halo against a garish test background, a soft (not razor) edge.
Worked example: the hard cutout
  1. Open a portrait with loose or curly hair against a reasonably plain background (or l03-curly-hair-portrait.jpg). 16-bit, Save As cutout-working.psd. Duplicate the layer so the original stays intact beneath, and hide the original.
  2. Rough in the mass. Select ▸ Subject. Tidy obvious misses with Quick Selection (paint to add, Alt/⌥ to subtract). Don't chase strands yet.
  3. Refine the hairline. Select ▸ Select and Mask (Ctrl/⌘ + Alt/⌥ + R). View: On Layers or Overlay. Edge Detection: tick Smart Radius, raise Radius a few pixels; try Refine Hair. Refine Edge Brush along the hairline. Smooth and Feather low; Shift Edge slightly negative.
  4. Rescue gaps. Output To: New Layer with Layer Mask (Decontaminate off). Build a channel mask of the hair (best channel → duplicate → Levels), Ctrl/⌘-click it, invert if needed. Click the layer mask thumbnail; paint white with a soft, low-opacity brush along the hairline.
  5. Decontaminate & pressure-test. With the rescued mask selected, reopen Select and Mask, tick Decontaminate Colors, raise Amount, output to New Layer with Layer Mask. Put a saturated color layer behind the cutout; fix any fringe; place your real background (l03-new-background.jpg). Save.
Recipe card: Hard Cutout
  1. Select ▸ Subject for the solid mass
  2. Select ▸ Select and Mask → Smart Radius on, Refine Edge Brush along the hairline; low Smooth/Feather, Shift Edge slightly negative; output New Layer with Layer Mask
  3. Rescue missed strands: load a channel mask as a selection, paint white into the layer mask
  4. Final pass: reopen Select and Mask on the rescued mask, tick Decontaminate Colors, output to New Layer with Layer Mask
  5. Test against a saturated background; fix any fringe

Mantra: rough in, refine, rescue, decontaminate, in that order.

L04

Color Management & Working Spaces (2.1)

50 min
By the end you can
  • Explain working spaces (sRGB, Adobe RGB, ProPhoto) and pick the right one
  • Say why you edit in 16-bit and what banding actually is
  • Read the histogram and diagnose an image before touching a slider
  • Set up Photoshop's Color Settings once, correctly, for the rest of the course
Key ideas
  • An image is color in a container with two properties: how wide it is (the working space, or gamut) and how finely divided (the bit depth). 8-bit = 256 steps per channel; 16-bit = over 32,000.
  • Banding is what happens when an edit demands more tonal steps than the file has. 16-bit has about 128 times more steps, so gradients survive hard grading.
  • A number like "R 220" is meaningless without its container. Color management keeps track of the container so a color means the same thing from capture to screen to print.
  • Capture wide, edit wide and deep, convert down last. Never edit in the narrow export space.
  • Convert to Profile remaps the colors to fit the new space and keeps them looking right. Assign Profile just relabels the container and shifts your colors.

Worked example: set up & diagnose
  1. Edit ▸ Color Settings. RGB working space: Adobe RGB (1998) for print-capable work (or sRGB if you are strictly web). CMYK: your print provider's press profile (for example Coated GRACoL 2006 or Coated FOGRA39) or the default. Policies: Preserve Embedded Profiles, mismatch warnings on (Ask When Opening).
  2. Open your image (or l04-smooth-sky-16bit.tif). Confirm the bit depth: Image ▸ Mode ▸ 16 Bits/Channel for anything you will grade hard.
  3. Window ▸ Histogram. Before any edit: crammed left (underexposed) or right (overexposed)? A spike against either wall (clipped, lost detail)? Narrow (flat, low contrast)? Write one sentence: what does the image need?
  4. Prove the container matters. Duplicate a smooth-gradient image; set one copy to 8-bit, keep the other 16-bit. Apply the same aggressive Curves to both, then a strong brighten. Zoom in: the 8-bit copy bands.
  5. Export correctly. Save the layered master (for example color-master.psd). For web: Image ▸ Duplicate (tick Duplicate Merged Layers Only), then on the copy Edit ▸ Convert to Profile ▸ sRGB, Image ▸ Mode ▸ 8 Bits/Channel, export. (File ▸ Export ▸ Export As can also convert to sRGB for you.)

  3. Read the histogram and name the problem before editing

L05

Grading with Curves, Gradient Maps & LUTs (2.2)

60 min
By the end you can
  • Grade with per-channel Curves for precise color in shadows, mids and highlights
  • Use a Gradient Map to remap tones to a color ramp
  • Apply and save a LUT (Color Lookup) to reuse a grade consistently
  • Keep the whole grade non-destructive and dialable
Key ideas
  • Correcting asks "what is wrong?" Grading asks "what should it feel like?" Every grade is a remap: you decide what color each tone becomes.
  • Three remapping tools, from surgical to sweeping: per-channel Curves (the precision instrument), Gradient Map (map the whole tonal range to a ramp), LUT (a saved recipe of a finished grade).
  • Gradient Map: darkest pixels get the gradient's left color, brightest get its right color. Blend it, don't bake it: Color or Soft Light, opacity around 15–40%.
  • LUT (Look-Up Table, a .cube or .3dl file): "turn this input color into that output color," for every color. Apply one with a Color Lookup adjustment layer.
  • Correct, then grade, then bottle. Grading on top of a neutral base is what makes a look portable.
Worked example: grade & bottle it
  1. Open a portrait (or l05-portrait-studio.jpg) in your 2.1 pipeline: 16-bit, deliberate working space. Correct to a neutral base first (exposure and white balance) with a Curves layer or Filter ▸ Camera Raw Filter. Save As grade-working.psd.
  2. Contrast, then color. Curves layer: a gentle RGB S-curve. New Curves layer, Blue channel: lift the shadow end (blue shadows), drop the highlight end (warm, yellow highlights). For a truer teal, also lift Green slightly or drop Red slightly in the shadows.
  3. Gradient Map accent. Teal-to-gold ramp, layer set to Color or Soft Light, opacity about 20–30%.
  4. Refine locally. Mask a Curves or the Gradient Map layer so skin stays natural while the background goes moody. A luminosity mask (Lesson 1.1) keeps the grade out of the brightest highlights.
  5. Bottle it. Save grade-working.psd first. Disable the masks (Shift-click each mask thumbnail), hide anything that is not part of the grade, keep a Background layer plus adjustment layers, then File ▸ Export ▸ Color Lookup Tables → save a .cube. Re-enable the masks.
  6. Open a different photo (or l05-pier-dusk.jpg), add a Color Lookup layer, Load 3D LUT…, choose your .cube. Watch the whole look transfer.
Recipe card: Build a look and save it
  1. Curves: contrast (RGB S-curve) first
  2. Curves per channel: teal shadows, warm highlights (Blue channel split)

Key ideas

L07

Skin & Frequency Separation (3.1)

55 min
By the end you can
  • Explain what frequency separation splits an image into and why it matters
  • Build a correct low-frequency (tone/color) and high-frequency (texture) pair
  • Even out blotchy skin on the low layer without smearing pores
  • Remove a blemish on the high layer without leaving a soft patch
  • Know when frequency separation is the wrong tool
Key ideas
  • Skin is two things layered together. Low frequency: slow, gradual changes (cheek to shadow, redness, an uneven tan): tone and color. High frequency: fast, tiny changes (pores, fine hairs, grain): texture.
  • A blur or a soft brush destroys both at once: melted plastic. Frequency separation puts them on two layers so each edit never spills into the other.
  • Low = a copy blurred until the pores vanish but the features stay (often 4–8 px). High = Apply Image from Low, set to Linear Light. Together they look identical to the original.
  • Apply Image depends on bit depth. 8-bit: Blending Subtract, Scale 2, Offset 128. 16-bit (the course's recommendation): Blending Add, Invert ticked, Scale 2, Offset 0. There is no second radius to enter; Apply Image subtracts Low itself.
  • The radius follows pore size in pixels. A close beauty shot has bigger pores in the frame, so it needs a larger radius (and more restraint) than a candid at arm's length.
  • Group the pair and lower the group to about 70–85%. A little of the original showing through keeps it human.
Guided build: retouch the skin
  1. Open your portrait and do your global corrections first (Module 2). Check the bit depth in the title bar or Image ▸ Mode.
  2. Make the layers (10 min). Duplicate the layer twice (Ctrl/⌘ + J). Lower copy = Low, upper = High. Hide High. On Low: Filter ▸ Blur ▸ Gaussian Blur until the pores vanish but the features stay. Note the radius.
  3. Show and select High: Image ▸ Apply Image, Layer Low, Channel RGB. 16-bit: Add, Invert ✓, Scale 2, Offset 0. 8-bit: Subtract, Scale 2, Offset 128. Blend mode → Linear Light. ✅ Checkpoint: it looks normal again.
  4. Even the tone on Low (12 min). Big, soft Healing Brush (Sample: Current Layer) or a low-opacity Mixer Brush (Sample All Layers off). Push blotchy color toward its neighbors: redness by the nose, a harsh tan line, a muddy shadow.
  5. Remove blemishes on High (10 min). Healing Brush or Clone Stamp, Sample: Current Layer. Sample clean texture near a blemish and heal over it.
  6. Dial it back (5 min). Group both layers (Ctrl/⌘ + G), lower the group to 70–85%. Zoom to 100%: real pores everywhere? Save as skin-retouched.psd with layers. Lesson 3.2 opens this file.

Dodge & Burn Sculpting (3.2)

50 min
By the end you can
  • Explain why dodge and burn adds form that smoothing can't
  • Build a non-destructive 50% gray Soft Light dodge and burn layer
  • Work at low flow to build light gradually instead of streaking it on
  • Sculpt the planes of a face: cheekbones, jaw, nose bridge, brow
  • Check your work in black and white to judge form without color
Key ideas
  • A camera flattens a face; our brains read depth from where light falls. Dodging lightens and pulls a plane toward the viewer. Burning darkens and pushes it back.
  • Low and slow. Brush over a plane many times at Flow 5–10%. Streaky dodge and burn is just impatient dodge and burn.
  • Paint on a 50% gray layer in Soft Light: gray is invisible, lighter than gray dodges, darker burns. Every stroke stays adjustable, reversible, and maskable. The Dodge and Burn tools edit pixels directly and are hard to undo.
  • Follow the light that's already there. You amplify it; you don't invent a new light source.
  • Color hides uneven sculpting: check form under a temporary Black & White adjustment layer. Finish by lowering the gray layer's opacity until the effect is felt, not seen.
Guided build: sculpt the face
  1. Open skin-retouched.psd from L07 and select the top of the stack so the new layer lands above the retouch (above the L07 group, not inside it).
  2. Add the gray layer (3 min). Layer ▸ New ▸ Layer, Mode Soft Light, tick Fill with Soft-Light-neutral color (50% gray). Soft round brush, Hardness 0, Flow 5–10%, Opacity 100%. White = dodge, black = burn (X swaps).
  3. Burn the recesses (12 min). Black: eye sockets, sides of the nose, under the cheekbones, under the jaw. Many light passes.
  4. Dodge the highlights (12 min). White: tops of the cheekbones, bridge and tip of the nose, center of the forehead, brow bone. Amplify the existing light.
  5. Judge and temper (6 min). Toggle a temporary Black & White adjustment layer on top, fix lumps and streaks, turn it off. Lower the gray layer's opacity until the sculpting is felt, not seen. Delete the B&W layer and save as portrait-sculpted.psd. Lesson 3.3 finishes this file.

Eyes, Teeth & Hair: Natural Finish (3.3)

55 min
By the end you can
  • Brighten and sharpen eyes with a masked local edit
  • Whiten teeth by removing yellow first, then brightening, not the reverse
  • Tame flyaway hair and fill gaps without a helmet-hair look
  • Apply every finish through a mask so nothing bleeds where it shouldn't
  • Judge the "natural line": the point where more becomes worse
Key ideas
  • The last five percent (brighter eyes, cleaner teeth, tamed hair) is what people actually notice. Restrained, it lifts a portrait; overdone, it screams "edited."
  • Every finish has one shape: make an adjustment, hide it behind a black mask, paint it back only where it belongs. Nothing global, everything local.
  • Teeth: yellow out first, brightness second. Brightening a yellow tooth gives you a bright yellow tooth.
  • Real eye whites are faintly gray or pink; real teeth are faintly warm. Aim for clean, never glowing. The catchlight is sacred.
  • Hair: clone flyaways out and fill gaps on their own layer, following the hair's flow, and leave a few strays. Perfectly smooth hair looks like a wig.
  • The pipeline order is the workflow: global corrections → frequency separation → dodge & burn → eyes/teeth/hair.
Guided build: finish the portrait
  1. Open portrait-sculpted.psd from L08.
  2. Eyes (12 min). Curves layer, lift the midtones a little. Invert the mask (Ctrl/⌘ + I), paint white over the iris and whites with a small soft brush. Optional sharpen: merged copy (Ctrl + Alt + Shift + E / ⌘ + ⌥ + ⇧ + E), Filter ▸ Other ▸ High Pass about 1–3 px, blend Overlay or Soft Light; Alt/⌥-click Add Layer Mask for a black mask, paint white over the iris only. Never paint out the catchlight.
  3. Teeth (10 min). Hue/Saturation layer: Yellows, drop the saturation; then Master, raise the lightness slightly. Invert the mask, paint the teeth only, feather the gum line.
  4. Hair (10 min). Empty layer, Clone/Heal with Sample: All Layers. Short strokes that follow the hair remove flyaways against the background; clone nearby strands along their flow to fill a thin gap. Leave a few strays.
  5. Final review (8 min). Fit to screen, View ▸ Flip Horizontal (it flips only the view; choose it again to return). Toggle all your Module 3 work off and on. If the before looks untouched and the after just looks like a good day, you've nailed the natural line. Save the finished layered file under a new name (for example portrait-finished.psd). Deliver. 🏆

L10

Match Perspective, Scale & Lens (4.1)

55 min
By the end you can
  • Find a photo's horizon line and read the camera's eye level from it
  • Match an element's eye level and vanishing points to the background
  • Scale an element with the ground-contact rule, not guesswork
  • Recognize and match a lens: wide vs long, and the distortion each brings
  • Correct a mismatch with Perspective Warp and Free Transform
Key ideas
  • Every photo has one horizon at the camera's eye level (the height the lens was at), visible or not. Every added element must share it.
  • Find it from the scene's own lines. Receding parallel edges (road edges, window rows, a table's sides) converge on a vanishing point, and that point sits on the horizon.
  • Scale by the ground, not the eye. For people of similar height on the same flat ground, the horizon crosses each of them at the same body height (the eyes, when the camera was at standing eye level). Set the feet first; the distance then sets the size.
  • Lens character: a wide lens (e.g. 24mm) exaggerates depth and bends verticals near the edges; a long lens (e.g. 200mm) compresses depth and flattens features. Warp fixes geometry, not optics.
Worked example: the plaza practice file

In l10-plaza-background.jpg the tile grout, the window rows and the lamp-post line all converge on one point near the middle of the frame. A horizontal guide through that point is the horizon. Check it: the three people already in the plaza (red jacket near, blue coat middle, green top far) all have their eyes on that guide.

  1. New empty layer. Line tool (or Pen) along two receding edges, extended until they meet. Show rulers with Ctrl+R / ⌘+R; drag a horizontal guide through the meeting point. (Guides can only be horizontal or vertical, so they can't follow an angled edge.)
  2. Place the figure. Choose the tile where the shoes touch the ground; that contact point is now fixed.
  3. Ctrl+T / ⌘+T, scale from the feet until the guide crosses the eyes. Scaling is proportional by default in current versions, so don't hold Shift (it now switches proportional scaling off).
  4. Boxes, signs, buildings: Convert to Smart Object, Edit ▸ Perspective Warp: Layout mode, draw a quad on each face; Warp mode, drag the pins toward the vanishing point. People: a touch of Free Transform ▸ Distort for the lean is usually enough.
  5. Step back. Flatter or rounder than the surroundings? A small Lens Correction or subtle warp. Save the layered PSD.

L11

Light, Shadows & Reflections (4.2)

60 min
By the end you can
  • Read a scene's light: direction, quality (hard/soft), and color
  • Relight an element with dodge & burn and masked color so it matches
  • Build a believable cast shadow (transform, blur, gradient opacity)
  • Add the tight contact shadow that glues feet to the floor
  • Fake a convincing reflection on a glossy surface
Key ideas
  • Read the light first. Where is it coming from (existing shadows point away from the source)? How hard (crisp shadow edges = hard, direct sun; soft edges = overcast or diffused)? What color (warm sun, cool shade, green from foliage)? The element must obey all three.
  • Two shadows, two jobs. The cast shadow tells you where the light is; the contact shadow tells you the object is touching the world. Skip either and something feels wrong.
  • Real shadows change along their length: crisp and dark where they touch the object, soft and pale where they end.
  • Shadows are colored, not black. Outdoor shadows lean cool/blue (lit by the sky); indoor shadows pick up the room's bounced color.
Worked example: building the cast shadow
  1. Ctrl/⌘-click the element's layer mask thumbnail (or the layer thumbnail if it has no mask) to load its silhouette.
  2. New layer below the element. Edit ▸ Fill with a very dark color, deselect, set the layer to Multiply. (Filling a new layer works even for a masked cut-out or a Smart Object.)
  3. Free Transform ▸ Distort/Skew it onto the ground, running away from the light.
  4. Blur that grows with distance: Filter ▸ Blur Gallery ▸ Tilt-Shift or Field Blur (sharp pins near the object, strong blur at the far end), or blur a duplicate heavily and blend the two with a gradient mask.
  5. Gradient mask so it fades out; tint it toward the scene's shadow color (cool/blue outdoors). Match its depth to the real shadows, then usually pull back 5–10%.

L12

Atmosphere & the Unifying Grade (4.3)

55 min
By the end you can
  • Use atmospheric perspective (haze, fading contrast) to build depth
  • Add fog, light rays, and dust that sit between elements, not just on top
  • Lay a single unifying grade over the whole stack
  • Explain why one grade over everything beats grading each piece
  • Deliver a finished, cohesive key visual
Key ideas
  • Distance has a look. The farther something is, the more air washes it out: contrast drops, colors desaturate and shift toward the sky's color (usually cooler and lighter), edges soften.
  • A background element as crisp and saturated as the foreground feels pasted forward. Push it back with lower contrast and a little haze.
  • Atmosphere goes between, grade goes on top. Haze sits above the far elements and below the subject and its shadows; the grade sits at the very top and rains down on everything.
  • Unify last, grade once. Get every piece neutral and correct first, then one grade. One dial beats twenty.

L13

Type Systems, Styles & Grids (Lesson 5.1)

55 min
By the end you can
  • Build a modular type scale and a clear hierarchy from it
  • Create and apply Paragraph and Character Styles
  • Turn on real OpenType: ligatures, true small caps, oldstyle figures
  • Set up columns and a baseline grid (the document grid, spaced at your leading)
  • Pair typefaces from Adobe Fonts with intent
Key ideas
  • Modular scale: one base size × one ratio (1.25, 1.333, 1.618). Assign roles to steps and never freehand a size again.
  • Four cheap levers of hierarchy: size, weight, color and space. Often weight or space says "this matters" better than size.
  • Styles bottle a spec (font, size, leading, tracking, color, OpenType) under a name. Edit the style once and every instance updates.
  • No faux styling. Faux Bold and Faux Italic smear a regular weight. Use a real Bold/Italic or choose another face.
  • Photoshop has no true baseline grid. Set the document grid to your body leading, give text that leading (or a multiple), and align each block's first baseline by eye.
  • Usually two typefaces: one display, one text, chosen to contrast.

Smart-Object Templates & Linked Assets (Lesson 5.2)

55 min
By the end you can
  • Explain what a Smart Object protects and why it is non-destructive
  • Choose between embedded and linked Smart Objects
  • Build a mockup template with a warped placeholder
  • Use Replace Contents and Place Linked to swap art in one move
  • Update one linked file and have every design using it refresh
Key ideas
  • A container you can refill. Transforms, warps, Smart Filters and layer styles live on the Smart Object; Replace Contents swaps what's inside and keeps all of them.
  • The catch: the container's transform is applied to whatever you swap in. Build placeholders at the exact pixel size (or at least the exact aspect ratio) of the art they'll hold.
  • Embedded = stored inside the PSD, each copy independent (one-off protection). Linked (File ▸ Place Linked) = points at a file on disk that many documents share (the backbone of a system).
  • Effects on the container, not the art. Warp the placeholder; put shadows on a Multiply layer and highlights on a Screen layer above it, clipped.
  • Link discipline. Don't move or rename linked files; File ▸ Package gathers them for handoff.
Worked example: a reusable mockup (the guided build)
  1. Place the placeholder. Open l14-mockup-poster-wall.jpg (or your own surface photo). Rectangle tool (U), click once and enter the art's exact size: 1200 × 1800 px for the practice designs. Right-click ▸ Convert to Smart Object. Free Transform (Ctrl/⌘+T) ▸ Warp (or Perspective Warp) to fit the surface.
  2. Light it into the scene. A layer set to Multiply above the placeholder: paint the surface's shadows and creases back over the art. A Screen layer for highlights. Clip both to the placeholder (Alt/⌥-click the line between the layers).
  3. Swap in real art. Double-click the placeholder (it opens in its own tab), place your design, save, close. Prefer Place Linked for art that several mockups share.
  4. Prove reuse. Layer ▸ Smart Objects ▸ Replace Contents with another design at the same pixel size. The perspective, shadows and highlights hold. Save as your template.

L16

Generative for Compositing (6.1)

55 min
By the end you can
  • Frame generative fill as a compositing tool, not a replacement for craft
  • Extend a background with Generative Expand to fit a layout
  • Remove distractions and fill gaps with Generative Fill + a prompt
  • Generate connective elements (shadow ground, foliage, debris) to sell a composite
  • Use Harmonize to match a placed element's light and color to its background
  • Judge, mask, and hand-finish generative output, and respect its limits and licensing
Key ideas
  • A fast, fallible assistant. Like a talented but literal intern: superb at plausible texture (sky, grass, pavement, walls) and removals, unreliable at specifics (hands, text, exact brands, precise counts).
  • The loop is never "generate and ship." It is generate → pick the best of three → mask and blend → hand-fix the tells.
  • Every result lands on a new generative layer, with variations in the Properties panel. Regenerate as often as you like; each set is kept. Never flatten until the seams are blended.
  • Short prompts, small selections. "grass", "clear blue sky", or empty to match. A whole complex scene in one go makes it flail.
  • Harmonize (Photoshop 2025 and later) generates versions of a placed element with its light, color, and shadows adjusted to the background. Compare, keep the best, mask back what it shouldn't change.
  • It won't fix a weak composite. Mismatched perspective, light, or scale (Module 4) are still yours to fix. Generation fills texture; it doesn't reason about your scene.
Worked example: Harmonize a composite

Open a cramped or messy composite: yours from Module 4, or the practice parts (l16-cramped-scene.jpg with l16-pasted-subject.png placed hard against the right edge, as in l16-cramped-composite-preview.jpg). Save as l16-composite-working.psd.

  1. Give it room. Crop tool (C), options bar Fill: Generative Expand. Drag the canvas edges outward, commit, leave the prompt empty, click Generate. Pick the cleanest variation in the Properties panel. Check the new edges at 100% (Ctrl/⌘ + 1) for repeats or seams. Tip: hide the pasted subject while you expand, so the model continues the scene, not the person.
  2. Remove distractions. Lasso a distracting object with a little margin (include its shadow). Generative Fill with an empty prompt. If a variation leaves a smudge, regenerate or clean it with the Healing Brush.
  3. Grow connective tissue. Select ground beneath the pasted subject and prompt something like gravel with small shadows, or generate foreground foliage to overlap an edge. Mask each generative layer so it only contributes where you want.
  4. Harmonize the subject. If it still looks lit by a different photo, select its layer and run Harmonize (contextual task bar or Properties panel). Compare the variations with the original; keep the best; mask back wherever it changed something it shouldn't (an edge, a face, a logo).
  5. Finish by hand. Matching grain and a touch of sharpening on the generated regions; dodge & burn to marry their light to the scene; one unifying grade over everything (Lesson 4.3).

Advanced Actions & Conditionals (6.2)

50 min
By the end you can
  • Record a clean, reusable Action and re-order or re-record steps
  • Insert stops, menu items, and modal pauses for input
  • Add a Conditional so one Action handles landscape and portrait differently
  • Batch an Action across a folder and save out automatically
  • Build a droplet, a drag-and-drop executable for your workflow
Key ideas
  • Action = a recorded macro. Conditional = an if inside it. Batch = the Action pointed at a folder. Droplet = that batch saved as an icon you drop files onto.
  • Record relative, not absolute. Image Size records fixed dimensions; File ▸ Automate ▸ Fit Image scales the long edge. Set Preferences ▸ Units & Rulers to Percent before recording crops and selections.
  • Actions capture what you did, not what you meant. Record on a representative file; test on your weirdest one.
  • Insert Conditional tests the document or active layer: Document Is Landscape, Portrait, or Square; Document Mode Is RGB or CMYK; Document Is 16 Bits Per Channel; Document Has Layers; Layer Is Type or Smart Object. No condition for pixel size or a layer's name.
  • A true batch asks no questions. Override Action "Save As" Commands, Suppress File Open Options Dialogs, Suppress Color Profile Warnings, Log Errors To File, and dialog toggles off.
Worked example: a Delivery Action

Use a folder of mixed landscape and portrait copies (practice: l17-input/), keep the watermark (l17-watermark.png) outside that folder, and make an empty output folder beside it.

  1. Record the core. Window ▸ Actions ▸ new set Delivery, new action Web Delivery, Record. File ▸ Automate ▸ Fit Image at 2000 × 2000 px (it scales the long edge to 2000), Unsharp Mask, Place your watermark PNG, Layer ▸ Flatten Image, Save As JPG. Stop recording.
  2. Add the branch. Set Preferences ▸ Units & Rulers to Percent, then record two tiny helper actions in the same set: Crop Wide and Crop Tall. In Web Delivery, select the first step, then panel menu ▸ Insert Conditional: If Document Is Landscape play Crop Wide, else Crop Tall. The conditional lands below the selected step, so drag it to the top: crop first, then Fit Image, so the final long edge is still 2000 px.
  3. Batch it. File ▸ Automate ▸ Batch: Action = Web Delivery, Source = your folder, Destination = an output folder, tick Override Action "Save As" Commands, set naming. Run, and watch landscape and portrait each crop correctly.
  4. Make a droplet. File ▸ Automate ▸ Create Droplet, point it at Web Delivery, save it to your desktop. Drag a folder of new images onto it.

Record Layer ▸ Flatten Image before Save As: while the watermark is its own layer, Save As won't offer JPEG.

Key ideas
  • Template + data. The template is your design with certain layers marked as variable; the data is a table where each column maps to a variable and each row is one output.
  • Exactly three kinds of variable: Text Replacement (swap the words in a type layer), Pixel Replacement (swap the image in a layer, with fit rules), Visibility (show or hide a layer).
  • The variable name binds. Each variable binds to the CSV column whose header matches its name. Names can't contain spaces and must start with a letter, underscore, or colon. Naming the layer the same is a helpful habit, not a requirement.
  • The CSV rules: UTF-8 with a header row; Visibility values true/false (Photoshop rejects yes/no); Pixel Replacement values as full file paths, or just file names with the images in the same folder as the CSV.
  • Export Data Sets as Files renders one PSD per row (for JPG or PNG, run the PSDs through an Action).
  • Scripting begins when you need loops, math, renaming by pattern, an API, or decisions based on pixel values. Photoshop runs ExtendScript (.jsx) and newer UXP scripts (.psjs) via File ▸ Scripts ▸ Browse.

Panoramas, HDR & Focus Stacking (7.1)

55 min
By the end you can
  • Shoot overlapping frames and stitch a panorama with Photomerge
  • Bracket exposures and combine them with Merge to HDR Pro for wider range
  • Shoot a focus series and focus-stack it with Auto-Align + Auto-Blend
  • See the shared engine (auto-align, then blend) behind all three
  • Clean the seams, ghosts, and edges these merges leave behind
Key ideas
  • Align, then blend. Auto-Align finds common detail and warps the frames into registration. Blend then chooses, per pixel, the best contribution: the overlap for a panorama, the well-exposed tone for HDR, the sharpest region for a stack.
  • Change one variable only. Angle for a panorama, shutter (exposure) for HDR, focus distance for a stack. Hold everything else as steady as you can; a tripod and a 2-second timer help most for HDR and stacking.
  • Three front doors: File ▸ Automate ▸ Photomerge; File ▸ Automate ▸ Merge to HDR Pro; File ▸ Scripts ▸ Load Files into Stack, then Edit ▸ Auto-Align Layers and Edit ▸ Auto-Blend Layers… (Stack Images).
  • HDR output: 16 Bit (or 8 Bit) tone-maps right in the dialog; 32 Bit keeps the full range to tone-map later with Camera Raw.
  • Garbage frames in, garbage merge out. Too little overlap won't stitch; movement mid-bracket leaves ghosts; a skipped focus distance leaves a soft band.
Worked example: merge three ways
  1. Panorama. File ▸ Automate ▸ Photomerge, Browse to the pan frames, Layout Auto, tick Blend Images Together and Content Aware Fill Transparent Areas, OK. Crop or accept the filled edges; heal any obvious stitch artifact.
  2. HDR. File ▸ Automate ▸ Merge to HDR Pro, add the bracket, tick Remove Ghosts if anything moved. For a finished file now, set Mode to 16 Bit and tone-map in the dialog (keep it believable). To keep every stop, set 32 Bit and tone-map later with Camera Raw.
  3. Focus stack. File ▸ Scripts ▸ Load Files into Stack (tick Attempt to Automatically Align Source Images). Select all layers, Edit ▸ Auto-Blend Layers…, choose Stack Images, tick Seamless Tones and Colors. Mask-fix any soft patch.
  4. Clean the joins. Zoom to 100% on each result and heal seams, ghosts, or soft bands. Apply a Module 4 unifying grade so all three feel like your work.

Shooting your own: overlap pan frames by roughly 30% (Photomerge doesn't measure it for you); bracket −2 / 0 / +2; step focus in small, even moves from front to back.

Timeline Animation & Export (7.2)

50 min
By the end you can
  • Choose between Frame Animation and the Video Timeline
  • Build a frame loop with tweening
  • Animate layer position, opacity, and style with keyframes (and scale, via Smart Objects)
  • Loop cleanly and shape timing so motion feels natural
  • Export a GIF (Save for Web) or an MP4 (Render Video)
Key ideas
  • Frame Animation is the flip-book: discrete frames, each a layer arrangement with its own hold time; Tween generates the in-betweens. Best for short, loopy GIFs with exact timing.
  • Video Timeline is the smooth path: each layer gets a track; click a property's stopwatch to drop a keyframe, move the playhead, change the value, and Photoshop adds the next keyframe and interpolates between them.
  • Tracks depend on the layer. Normal layers get Position, Opacity, and Style. Smart Objects get a Transform track instead of Position, which is how you animate scale and rotation. Warps can't be keyframed.
  • No built-in ease. Keyframes interpolate linearly (or Hold, for a sudden jump). Fake an ease with an extra keyframe near the end of a move.
  • Two doors out: File ▸ Export ▸ Save for Web (Legacy) for a GIF (256 colors max, heavy); File ▸ Export ▸ Render Video for an H.264 MP4 (smaller, full color).
Worked example: a looping animation
  1. Open a simple layered design (a logo, a badge, a headline). Convert anything you'll scale to a Smart Object.
  2. Start the timeline. Window ▸ Timeline, click Create Video Timeline. Panel menu ▸ Set Timeline Frame Rate ▸ 24 fps. Set a 2–3 s duration: drag the right end of each layer's bar to the 3-second mark, then drag the work-area end handle (above the ruler) to match.
  3. Keyframe the moves. On the badge (a Smart Object), twirl open and click the Transform stopwatch at frame 0 with the badge small. Move the playhead, scale it to its final size and spot; a keyframe drops automatically. On the headline, keyframe Opacity 0 → 100 and a small upward Position move so it rises in.
  4. Loop and shape the timing. Hold the finished pose for a beat, then add keyframes that return each layer to its frame-0 state by the last frame. To fake an ease, add an extra keyframe close to the end of each move with only a small change left. Play back and adjust until it feels natural.
  5. Export both. Save for Web (Legacy) (Ctrl+Alt+Shift+S / ⌘+⌥+Shift+S) → GIF, Looping Forever, tuned palette. Render Video → H.264. One animation, two delivery formats.

Studio Session: Build It (8.2)

self-paced · home + Session 8 studio
By the end you can
  • Execute a project in passes: block-in, refine, polish
  • Keep ruthless layer hygiene: groups, names, color labels
  • Stay non-destructive with Smart Objects and adjustment layers throughout
  • Version and save so you can always step back safely

Key ideas
  • Work in passes. Raise the whole image a level at a time. Each pass is done across the frame before you start the next, never one perfect corner at a time. Zoom out between passes and judge the frame, not the pixel.
  • Layer hygiene. Group by role, top-to-bottom in render order, name everything, color-label groups. Every adjustment a live layer, every scaled element a Smart Object, every filter a Smart Filter.
  • Save versions, and save often: capstone_v01, v02 at each pass, plus a flattened "reference" JPG of milestones.
  • Done is when a pass changes almost nothing and every brief success criterion is met, not when you run out of things you could do.

Key ideas
