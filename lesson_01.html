Module 1 • Lesson 1🌗 Luminosity Masks: Select by Tone

In the intro, a mask was a grayscale stencil you painted by hand. A luminosity mask is a stencil the image paints for you, from its own brightness. It is the professional way to adjust highlights and shadows separately, and it is the foundation of natural landscape and portrait work.

📚 What You'll Learn

By the end of this lesson, you will be able to:

  • Explain a luminosity mask: a selection built from the image's own luminance
  • Load a Lights or Darks selection from the channels
  • Adjust highlights and shadows independently, edges self-feathered and invisible
  • Narrow a mask to a tonal band (just the brightest highlights) by intersecting

⏱️ Estimated Time: 55 minutes

🎯 Project: Rescue a high-contrast landscape, recovering a blown sky and lifting a dark foreground without a single visible mask edge.

🏔️ The Goal: Two Exposures, One Frame

A scene the camera cannot hold in one shot: expose for the sky and the land goes black; expose for the land and the sky blows out. Luminosity masks let you treat each tonal range on its own. On the right, the raw high-contrast capture; on the left, the balanced result. Drag the handle:

🧠 Mental Model: The Image Paints the Mask

Recall the intro's rule: on a mask, white reveals and black hides. A luminosity mask simply says: let the picture's own brightness be that black-and-white stencil. Load a Lights mask and the bright pixels become white (selected, editable) while the dark pixels become black (protected). The mask is literally a grayscale copy of the photo's luminance.

Two things make this powerful:

  • The edges are perfect. Because the mask follows the actual tones, its transitions are exactly as soft as the light in the scene. No halos, no hand-feathering.
  • It is self-targeting. You never have to paint around the sky to darken it; the sky is the bright part, so a Lights mask already contains it.

🧠 The spiral, one turn deeper

You already know masks are grayscale and selections become masks (intro, Modules 1 and 4). Luminosity masking is those two ideas fused: a selection derived from the image, saved as a mask. Nothing new to believe, just a smarter source for the stencil.

🎚️ Lights, Midtones & Darks

Pros do not stop at one Lights mask. They build a small family of tonal selections and pick the one that matches the job. Think of the full tonal range as a bar from black to white, with overlapping selections living in different regions:

✅ Narrowing to a band

Need only the brightest highlights (a blown sky, not the whole upper half)? Load Lights, then intersect it with Lights again (Ctrl/⌘ + Shift + Alt/⌥-click the channel). Each intersection keeps only the region both masks share, pushing the selection further into the extreme tones. That is how you target a sky without touching a bright shirt below it.

🛠️ Guided Build: Rescue the Landscape

Open a high-contrast photo (a bright sky over a darker foreground is ideal). Convert it to 16-bit first (Image ▸ Mode ▸ 16 Bits/Channel) for smoother masks, then save as luminosity-working.psd. Converting does not add detail to an 8-bit original, but it stops new banding as you edit.

Step 1: Diagnose, then load Lights · 6 min

  1. Read the histogram. Confirm the highlights are piling up near the right (the bright sky) and shadows near the left (the dark land).
  2. Open the Channels panel and Ctrl/⌘-click the RGB thumbnail. Marching ants appear around the brighter half of the image.

Step 2: Tame the highlights through the mask · 8 min

  1. With that selection active, add a Curves adjustment layer. The Lights selection becomes its mask automatically.
  2. Pull the curve down to bring the sky back. Because the mask is the luminance, the darkening fades out naturally before it reaches the land.

⚠️ If it spills onto the land

Your Lights mask was too broad. Undo the layer, reload Lights, then intersect once or twice (Ctrl/⌘ + Shift + Alt/⌥-click RGB) to push the selection into just the brightest tones before adding the adjustment.

Step 3: Lift the shadows through a Darks mask · 8 min

  1. Reload the RGB luminosity selection, then invert it (Ctrl/⌘ + Shift + I) so the dark land is now selected.
  2. Add another Curves layer and lift the shadows. The foreground opens up; the already-correct sky is protected because it is black on this mask.

✅ Why this beats one global Curves

A single S-curve would brighten the sky while it lifts the land, undoing your highlight recovery. Two luminosity-masked curves move highlights and shadows in opposite directions at once. That is a range only masks can reach.

Step 4: Check the edges & finish · 6 min

  1. Zoom to 100% along the horizon. A luminosity-masked edit should have no visible seam where sky meets land. If you see a glow, your masks overlap; reduce one layer's opacity or re-intersect.
  2. Optional: a gentle global Curves on top for overall contrast. Save the layered master.

✅ Project Completion Checklist

  • ☐ Highlights recovered through a Lights (RGB) mask
  • ☐ Shadows lifted through an inverted Darks mask
  • ☐ No visible seam or halo at the horizon at 100%
  • ☐ Every edit is a non-destructive adjustment layer with a luminosity mask
  • ☐ Worked in 16-bit where possible

🧗 Now You: Solo Variation

🌟 Your challenge

  1. On a portrait or still life, load a Lights mask and add a subtle glow by lifting only the brightest highlights. Notice how it never touches the shadows.
  2. Build a Midtones mask (save Lights and Darks as channels with Select ▸ Save Selection, then Select All and Ctrl/⌘ + Alt/⌥-click each saved channel to subtract it) and add contrast to only the mids, a classic way to add depth without clipping. (Lights minus Darks is not midtones: it narrows to the brightest tones, like a second intersect.)
  3. Compare a luminosity-masked sky darken against a hand-painted mask on the same image. The self-feathered edge is the whole point.

Going further: the pros automate this. Record an Action that loads Lights, Darks, and Midtones as saved channel masks in one click, or try a free luminosity-mask panel. You will reuse these selections constantly.

🍳 Recipe Card: Luminosity Mask

Adjust highlights and shadows separately

  1. Channels panel: Ctrl/⌘-click RGB = Lights selection
  2. Add a Curves layer → the selection becomes its mask
  3. Too broad? Intersect (Ctrl/⌘ + Shift + Alt/⌥-click RGB) to reach extreme tones
  4. Shadows: reload, invert (Ctrl/⌘ + Shift + I), add another Curves
  5. Check the horizon at 100% for seams; work in 16-bit

Mantra: let the image paint the mask.

📝 Lesson Summary

🎓 Key Takeaways

  • A luminosity mask is a selection built from the image's own brightness.
  • Ctrl/⌘-click RGB loads Lights; invert for Darks; intersect to reach extreme bands.
  • Its edges self-feather to the scene's light, so there are no halos.
  • Two masked curves move highlights and shadows in opposite directions at once.

🎉 What You've Accomplished

You just used the technique that underpins virtually all high-end landscape and natural-light work. Luminosity masking is the difference between "I brightened the photo" and "I placed light exactly where it belonged," and you will lean on it in the color, retouching, and compositing modules ahead.

❓ Common Questions at This Stage

Is a luminosity mask the same as the "Blend If" sliders?

They are cousins — both target tones — but Blend If acts per layer at blend time and is instant, while a luminosity mask is an explicit, editable selection you can refine and reuse. You will meet Blend If in Lesson 1.2; often you will use both.

Why load from RGB instead of a single channel?

RGB gives the true perceived luminance. Individual channels (Red, Green, Blue) make great specialized masks — the Blue channel is famous for isolating skies and skin — which is exactly where Lesson 1.2 goes next.

My masks look blocky or banded.

You are almost certainly in 8-bit. Convert to 16-bit (Image ▸ Mode) before building luminosity masks. Converting cannot restore detail an 8-bit file never had, but the extra tonal resolution keeps new masks and adjustments smooth.
