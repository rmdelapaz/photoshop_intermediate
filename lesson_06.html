👤 The Goal: A Finished, Portable Look

A neutral studio portrait on the right; the same shot split-toned on the left, with cooler shadows and warmer highlights for a polished, editorial feel. Drag the handle:

🧠 Mental Model: Split-Tone by Zone

A split-tone puts one color into the shadows and a different (usually complementary) color into the highlights. The most flattering and cinematic version is cool shadows, warm highlights — it mimics warm sunlight and cool skylight, which is how our eyes expect the world to look. It is the backbone of most film and portrait grades.

You control the two zones separately. Color Balance has explicit Shadows / Midtones / Highlights radio buttons; per-channel Curves do the same by which end of the curve you bend. Either way, the mental move is: decide a shadow color and a highlight color, then push each into its zone.

🔗 Matching Two Images

A gallery, a product set, or a magazine spread has to feel like one shoot even when the frames were lit differently. To match a photo to a reference:

  1. Put both images side by side and pick the one whose look you want as the reference.
  2. Sample a known neutral (or a skin tone) in each with the Color Sampler Tool and read the RGB values in the Info panel (Window ▸ Info). The differences tell you which way each channel must move.
  3. On the image you are matching, add Curves and nudge each channel until its sampled values meet the reference's. Match the black point, white point, and mid color in that order.

✅ Match tones before colors

Get the two images to the same brightness and contrast first; a color match laid over mismatched exposure never holds. There is also Image ▸ Adjustments ▸ Match Color for a quick automatic pass, but hand-matching with Curves is more controllable and is what survives client scrutiny.

🖨️ Soft-Proofing for Print

Your screen glows in RGB; paper reflects CMYK ink and cannot reproduce every screen color. Soft proofing simulates the print on your monitor before you commit, so there are no surprises. Turn it on with View ▸ Proof Setup ▸ Custom:

⚠️ Neon dies on paper

The most common print letdown is a vivid, saturated color (a neon sunset, an electric blue) that the gamut warning lights up. Pull those areas' saturation down until the warning clears, and the print will match your soft-proof instead of disappointing you.

🛠️ Guided Build: Split-Tone & Proof

Open grade-working.psd from Lesson 2.2 (16-bit, wide space). The skin checks below assume a portrait; if you graded a landscape there, drag your Lesson 2.2 grading layers onto a corrected portrait instead. Save as finish-working.psd.

Step 1: Split-tone it · 10 min

  1. Add a Color Balance layer with Preserve Luminosity ticked. On Shadows, push toward cyan/blue a little. On Highlights, push toward red/yellow. Leave Midtones near neutral.
  2. Judge on skin: it should look warmly lit, not sunburnt. Ease off until it is believable.

Step 2: Match a companion image · 10 min

  1. Open a second photo that should belong to the same set. Drop a Color Sampler on a comparable point in each.
  2. Add Curves to the second image and move each channel until its sampled values approach the first image's. Now they read as one shoot.

Step 3: Soft-proof · 8 min

  1. View ▸ Proof Setup ▸ Custom, choose your printer's profile (or U.S. Web Coated (SWOP) v2 as a stand-in), set Rendering Intent to Relative Colorimetric or Perceptual with Black Point Compensation on, tick Simulate Paper Color, and turn on proof (Ctrl/⌘ + Y). The image dulls slightly — that is the paper.
  2. Turn on Gamut Warning (Ctrl/⌘ + Shift + Y). Anything grayed is out of gamut; lower its saturation (a masked Hue/Saturation) until it clears.

Step 4: Deliver both files · 6 min

  1. Save the layered master first; it stays wide and 16-bit.
  2. Web: Image ▸ Duplicate (Duplicate Merged Layers Only), then on the copy Convert to Profile ▸ sRGB, set 8 Bits/Channel, and export a JPG.
  3. Print: make another merged duplicate, Convert to Profile ▸ your printer/CMYK profile (or keep tagged RGB if your printer asks for it), and save it as PDF or TIFF. Module 2 deliverable: one look, delivered correctly for two destinations. 🏆

✅ Project Completion Checklist

  • ☐ A believable split-tone (cool shadows, warm highlights, neutral-ish mids)
  • ☐ A second image matched to read as the same set
  • ☐ Soft-proofed with a deliberate rendering intent and paper simulation; gamut warnings resolved
  • ☐ Delivered a web (sRGB) and a print (CMYK/printer-profile) file
  • ☐ Layered master kept wide + 16-bit

🧗 Now You: Solo Variation

🌟 Your challenge

  1. Match a set of three photos shot in different light so they could sit together on a page.
  2. Split-tone the same image two ways: the cinematic cool/warm, and an unusual pairing (green shadows, magenta highlights). Feel how the pairing changes the emotion.
  3. Soft-proof a deliberately vivid image and watch how much saturation you must surrender for print. That gap is why print work is graded differently from web.

Going further: save your split-tone as a LUT (Lesson 2.2) so it and your soft-proof workflow become a repeatable delivery pipeline.

🍳 Recipe Card: Finish & Proof

Split-tone, match, and print-check

  1. Color Balance: cool shadows, warm highlights, neutral mids
  2. Match a companion image with Color Sampler + Curves (tones before colors)
  3. View ▸ Proof Setup ▸ Custom → printer profile, Rendering Intent + Black Point Compensation, Simulate Paper Color
  4. Gamut Warning (Ctrl/⌘ + Shift + Y) → desaturate flagged areas
  5. Deliver from duplicates: Convert to sRGB (web) and to the printer profile (print)

Mantra: what you see is only trustworthy once you have proofed it.

📝 Module 2 Summary

🎓 Key Takeaways

  • A split-tone puts one color in the shadows, its complement in the highlights.
  • Match images by aligning tones first, then sampled channel values.
  • Soft-proof to preview print; the gamut warning flags impossible colors.
  • Deliver a look correctly for each destination: sRGB for web, the printer profile for print.

❓ Common Questions at This Stage

Do I convert to CMYK myself or leave it to the printer?

Ask the printer. Many prefer you deliver a tagged RGB (often Adobe RGB) file and let their calibrated RIP convert. Soft-proofing to their profile still tells you what to expect either way.

My split-tone makes skin look ill.

Your color is reaching the midtones where skin lives. Keep the split in the extremes, ease the amount, and mask the grade off the face with a subject or luminosity mask if needed.
