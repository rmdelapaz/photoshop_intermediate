Module 2 • Lesson 2🎬 Grading with Curves, Gradient Maps & LUTs

Color grading is where a correct photo becomes a photo with a mood. In the intro you met the S-curve; now you will build full cinematic looks with per-channel Curves and gradient maps, then bottle a look as a LUT you can pour over an entire shoot in one click.

By the end of this lesson, you will be able to:

  • Use a Gradient Map to remap tones to a color ramp
  • Apply and save a LUT (Color Lookup) to reuse a grade consistently
  • Keep the whole grade non-destructive and dialable

🎞️ The Goal: A Look, Not Just a Fix

🧠 Mental Model: Remapping Color

Correcting an image asks "what is wrong?" Grading asks "what should it feel like?" Technically, every grade is a remap: you decide what color each tone becomes. Shadows do not have to stay neutral; you can send them teal. Highlights can lean gold. That deliberate reassignment is the whole art.

Three tools do the remapping, from most surgical to most sweeping:

  • Per-channel Curves — the precision instrument. Bend the Red, Green, or Blue curve in the shadows or highlights to push exact color into exact tones.
  • Gradient Map — map the whole tonal range onto a chosen color ramp at once. Fast, bold, great for stylized looks.
  • LUT (Color Lookup) — a saved recipe of a finished grade you can re-apply instantly to any image.

🧠 The spiral from the intro

You already S-curved for contrast and split the Blue channel for teal-and-orange. This lesson formalizes that into a repeatable grading craft and, crucially, teaches you to save the look so it is not a one-off you can never reproduce.

🌗 Gradient Maps

A Gradient Map takes your image's tones and replaces them with the colors of a gradient: the darkest pixels get the gradient's left color, the brightest get its right color, everything else in between. Choose a teal-to-gold gradient and you have an instant cinematic split-tone.

⚠️ Blend it, don't bake it

At 100% Normal a Gradient Map looks garish and kills detail. Drop the opacity to 15–40%, or switch the layer to Color or Soft Light, so it lends its palette to the real image underneath.

🧴 LUTs: Bottling a Grade

A LUT (Look-Up Table, a .cube or .3dl file) is a saved recipe that says "turn this input color into that output color," for every color. Build a grade you love, export it as a LUT, and you can pour the exact look onto a hundred other photos, or hand it to a teammate. Apply one with a Color Lookup adjustment layer:

🛠️ Guided Build: Grade & Bottle It

Open a corrected, neutral image (from Lesson 2.1), 16-bit. Grade in this order.

Step 1: Contrast, then color · 10 min

  1. Add a Curves layer and set your contrast with a gentle RGB S-curve.
  2. New Curves layer. Switch to the Blue channel: lift the shadow end (teal shadows), drop the highlight end (warm highlights). Nudge Red and Green in tiny amounts to taste. This is the classic teal-and-gold in Curves.

Step 2: A Gradient Map accent · 8 min

  1. Add a Gradient Map with a teal-to-gold ramp.
  2. Set the layer to Color or Soft Light and drop opacity to ~20–30% so it unifies the palette without flattening.

Step 3: Refine locally · 6 min

  1. Grades rarely suit every part of a frame. Mask a Curves or the Gradient Map layer (Module 1 skills) so, say, skin stays natural while the background goes moody.
  2. A luminosity mask (Lesson 1.1) is perfect for keeping the grade out of the brightest highlights.

Step 4: Bottle it as a LUT · 6 min

  1. With your grading layers active (over a neutral base, no image-specific masks), choose File ▸ Export ▸ Color Lookup Tables and save a .cube.
  2. Open a different photo, add a Color Lookup layer, load your LUT, and watch the whole look transfer. That is your grade, now reusable forever. 🏆

✅ Why bottle it

A LUT is how a look becomes a brand: the same feel across an entire shoot, a client's whole feed, or a team's output. Building the grade is craft; saving it is professionalism.

✅ Project Completion Checklist

  • ☐ Contrast set before color
  • ☐ Color graded with per-channel Curves
  • ☐ A Gradient Map used at reduced opacity / a blend mode
  • ☐ Grade refined with at least one mask
  • ☐ Look exported as a LUT and re-applied to a second image

🧗 Now You: Solo Variation

🌟 Your challenge

  1. Build three distinct LUTs: a warm-summer look, a cold-thriller look, and a faded-matte look. Apply each to the same photo to feel how much mood is pure color.
  2. Reverse-engineer a film still or a favorite photo: name its shadow color and highlight color, then recreate it with per-channel Curves.
  3. Apply one of your LUTs across five different photos and note where it needs per-image tweaking. A LUT is a starting point, not a finisher.

Going further: stack a subtle Gradient Map under a Color Lookup and mask both — layered grading is how the richest looks are built.

🍳 Recipe Card: Cinematic Grade

Build a look and save it

  1. Curves: contrast (RGB S-curve) first
  2. Curves per channel: teal shadows, warm highlights (Blue channel split)
  3. Gradient Map (teal→gold) at ~20–30% / Color blend
  4. Mask locally (luminosity or subject masks)
  5. File ▸ Export ▸ Color Lookup Tables → reuse as a .cube

Mantra: correct, then grade, then bottle.

📝 Lesson Summary

🎓 Key Takeaways

  • Grading is deliberate color remapping: tones become chosen colors.
  • Per-channel Curves are the precision tool; Gradient Maps are the fast, bold one.
  • Blend a Gradient Map (low opacity / Color mode); never bake it at 100%.
  • Save a look as a LUT to reuse it across a shoot or a brand.

🎉 What You've Accomplished

You can now author a look, not just fix an image — and, just as importantly, save it so it is repeatable. That combination of taste plus reusability is exactly what studios and clients pay for.

❓ Common Questions at This Stage

Gradient Map vs a LUT — when do I use which?

A Gradient Map is a live tool you build inside one document. A LUT is a saved, portable file. Often you build a look with Curves + Gradient Maps, then export that look as a LUT to reuse. They are stages of the same craft.

My grade looks great on this photo but wrong on others.

A LUT assumes a similar starting point. Correct each image to a neutral base first (Module 2.1), then apply the LUT. Grading un-corrected images is why a look "breaks" from photo to photo.
